📘Introduction

Objectives

  • Establish the equivalent circuit of a line, a cable and a transformer from manufacturer data, and choose the model (short, medium, long) suited to its length.
  • Calculate the natural power of a line, its Ferranti effect and the compensation required.
  • Convert physical quantities to per-unit quantities and back.
  • Calculate a voltage drop and losses; check a voltage profile.
  • Write the load-flow equations, do one Gauss-Seidel iteration by hand and interpret the convergence of the three methods.
  • Calculate three-phase, phase-to-phase and phase-to-earth short-circuit currents according to IEC 60909 and deduce the breaking capacity.
  • Dispatch generation among several plants at least cost (equal incremental costs, limits, losses).
  • Represent a synchronous machine (Xd, X′d, X″d, capability curve) and an induction machine (slip, torque, starting) and deduce their effect on voltage and short circuit.
  • Set and check a protection (inverse-time curve, time grading, distance, differential), a CT and a surge arrester.
  • Compare neutral earthing systems (isolated, resistance, compensated, solid): fault current, overvoltages, Petersen coil.

Prerequisites: level 1, elementary matrix calculus. Duration: 24 to 30 h.

🧵2.1 Lines and cables

A line or cable is characterised by four per-length quantities (per km): resistance r, inductance l (reactance x = lω), capacitance c and conductance g (neglected). Orders of magnitude:

Long line: distributed parameters

On a slice dx, dV/dx = z·I and dI/dx = y·V, hence d²V/dx² = zy·V and the travelling-wave solution:

γ = √(zy) = √((r + jLω)(g + jCω)) = α + jβ     Zc = √(z/y)
V(x) = cosh(γx)·VR + Zc·sinh(γx)·IR    I(x) = sinh(γx)/Zc·VR + cosh(γx)·IR

γ is the propagation constant (α: attenuation, β: phase shift), Zc the characteristic impedance (or surge impedance). A long line can always be reduced to an exact π: Z′ = Zc·sinh(γℓ) and Y′/2 = tanh(γℓ/2)/Zc.

Lossless line and natural power (SIL)

For r = g = 0: α = 0, β = ω√(LC) and Zc = √(L/C) is real (≈ 400 Ω for an overhead line, 30 to 50 Ω for a cable). If the line delivers exactly into Zc, the current is VR/Zc:

SIL = 3·VR²/Zc = Un²/Zc     (natural power, surge impedance loading)

In this condition, |V(x)| and |I(x)| are constant along the line and QS = QR = 0: the reactive power consumed by the series inductance is exactly supplied by the shunt capacitance. This is the reference equilibrium point:

LoadBehaviourRemedy
= SILflat voltage profile, no reactive exchange—
< SIL (or no-load line)the line produces reactive power: voltage rise at the receiving endshunt reactors (inductors)
> SILthe line consumes reactive power: significant voltage sagshunt capacitors, static compensators, series capacitors
Voltage profile along a line depending on the load. At natural power the profile is flat; below it, the voltage rises (hence shunt reactors); above it, it drops (shunt or series capacitors, static compensators).

Ferranti effect: the extreme case of the long line at no load (IR = 0). Then VR = VS/cosh(γℓ): the voltage at the end of the line is higher than at the sending end (the leading capacitive current “pushes up” the voltage through the series inductance). Hence the operating rule: a long EHV line is never left energised at no load without a compensating reactor.

🔸2.2 Transformers

The Kapp equivalent circuit reduces the transformer to a series impedance Zsc = Rsc + jXsc and a magnetising branch (Rfe ∥ jXm), from the short-circuit test (usc, Psc) and the no-load test (i₀, P₀):

Zsc = usc·U²/Sn    Rsc = Psc·U²/Sn²    Xsc = √(Zsc² − Rsc²)    (U = voltage of the side to which the impedance is referred)

The vector group (Dyn, YNd, Yyn…) plays no role in the positive sequence but determines the zero-sequence path: a delta winding blocks the zero-sequence current, an earthed star neutral lets it through. Dyn11, very common in distribution, isolates the MV network from LV earth faults and provides the LV network with an earthed neutral.

Example 2.1 — Distribution transformer

250 kVA, 30 kV / 400 V, usc = 4 %, Psc = 3.25 kW. Calculate Zsc, Rsc, Xsc referred to the secondary, the rated current and the voltage drop at full load with cos φ = 0.8.

Show the solution

Zsc = 0.04 × 400²/250 000 = 25.6 mΩ; Rsc = 3 250 × 400²/250 000² = 8.32 mΩ; Xsc = 24.21 mΩ. In = 250 000/(√3 × 400) = 360.8 A.

ΔU ≈ √3·I·(R cos φ + X sin φ) = √3 × 360.8 × (0.00832 × 0.8 + 0.02421 × 0.6) = 13.2 V, i.e. 3.3 %.

These are exactly the values computed by the parameter dialog of the NRJXpert three-phase transformer (Network palette).

📗2.3 Per-unit system

A base power Sb (common to the whole network, e.g. 100 MVA or 10 MVA) and a base voltage Ub per voltage level (transformer ratios) are chosen. Every quantity is divided by its base:

Zb = Ub²/Sb    Ib = Sb/(√3·Ub)    zpu = Z/Zb    change of base: z₂ = z₁·(Sb2/Sb1)·(Ub1/Ub2)²

Advantages: transformers disappear (ratio 1 in pu), voltages are close to 1 pu, orders of magnitude can be compared between networks (the usc of a transformer = its impedance in pu on its own base). The NRJXpert server works in pu for the load flow (Sb = 100 MVA, Ub = network voltage entered in the calculation settings).

Why two base quantities are enough

Four complex quantities describe a three-phase circuit: the line voltage U, the line current I, the apparent power S and the impedance Z. They are related by U = √3V, S = 3V·I* and V = Z·I: only two are independent. We therefore choose Sb and Ub, and deduce the rest:

Ib = Sb/(√3·Ub)    Vb = Ub/√3 = Zb·Ib    Zb = Ub²/Sb

In per-unit, the factors 3 and √3 disappear: s = v·i*, v = z·i. The circuit laws remain valid.

The transformer disappears

If the base voltages are chosen in the transformation ratio (Ub2 = Ub1/m) and the same base power on both sides, then v′₂pu = v₂pu and i′₂pu = i₂pu: the transformer reduces to its short-circuit impedance alone, and the whole network becomes a circuit at a single voltage level. This is the raison d'être of the per-unit system. (Since NRJXpert does not yet represent the transformation ratio in the calculation, impedances must be referred to the voltage of the point studied: see § 2.2 and the limits of the three-phase transformer.)

Ohms, percentage, per-unit

%z = 100·Z·S/U²     Z = %z·U²/(100·S) = zpu·U²/S     zpu = %z/100     ZU2 = ZU1·(U₂/U₁)²

Manufacturers give the impedances of machines and transformers in % (or in pu) on their own base: usc = 6 % means z = 0.06 pu at rated power. A network study requires bringing everything to a common base: znew = zold·(Sb,new/Sb,old)·(Ub,old/Ub,new)².

📉2.4 Voltage drop and losses

For a line of impedance R + jX carrying P + jQ at line voltage U:

ΔU/U ≈ (R·P + X·Q)/U²    losses: ΔP = R·(P² + Q²)/U² = 3·R·I²

Practical consequences: the voltage drop depends mainly on P in resistive networks (LV, MV with small cross-sections) and on Q in inductive networks (HV); hence voltage control through reactive power in transmission, and the role of controlled inverters in distribution (level 5). Losses vary as the square of the power and the inverse square of the voltage: doubling the voltage divides losses by 4.

Example 2.2 — MV feeder

A 10 km, 20 kV line (r = 0.3 Ω/km, x = 0.35 Ω/km) supplies 2 MW at cos φ = 0.9 at its end. Voltage drop and losses?

Show the solution

R = 3 Ω, X = 3.5 Ω, Q = 2 × tan(25.84°) = 0.969 Mvar. ΔU/U = (3 × 2 + 3.5 × 0.969)/20² = 2.35 % (470 V); losses = 3 × (2² + 0.969²)/20² = 0.037 MW = 37 kW (1.9 %).

Reactive power compensation

A shunt compensator placed near the loads supplies locally the reactive power they consume: the reactive power flowing in the lines decreases, and with it the voltage drop (X·Q term) and the losses (R·Q² term). A capacitor bank with n steps switched on supplies Q = n·Qstep·V² (constant impedance: it supplies less when the voltage falls). Shunt reactors absorb reactive power: they lower the voltage of lightly loaded or highly capacitive networks (long cables). With automatic control, a step is switched on when the voltage leaves the dead band from below and switched off when it leaves it from above; the band must be wider than the voltage change caused by one step, otherwise the steps hunt (palette: Power system (load flow) › Switched shunt compensator).

Voltage control with transformer taps

The substation transformer is the most common means of holding the voltage of a feeder. Its primary winding carries taps that change the number of turns: the ratio becomes a = 1 + n·p (n = tap position, p = step, often 1.25 %). A positive position puts more turns on the primary and lowers the secondary voltage; a negative one raises it. An on-load tap changer changes position under load, one step at a time, when the regulated voltage leaves the dead band around the setpoint. In NRJXpert the ratio enters the transformer's π model (load flow, short circuit, dynamics); the dialog of the three-phase transformer (“Tap changer (OLTC)” section) offers “Nominal ratio”, “Fixed tap” or “Automatic (voltage control)”. In automatic mode the calculation moves one position at a time until the voltage on the chosen side is inside the band; it reports the limits and hunting (dead band narrower than the effect of one step). The clock-number phase shift can be added on request (ignored by the three-phase distribution sweep). A tap changer alone only corrects the head-of-feeder voltage: it raises or lowers the whole feeder by the same percentage, without reducing the voltage drop along the feeder (which compensators do).

🔀2.5 Load flow: formulation

The load flow (power flow) calculates the steady state of a network from the generated and consumed powers. Since powers rather than impedances are imposed, the equations are non-linear.

Admittance matrix and equations

Ybus is built element by element: for a branch of admittance yik between i and k, yik is added to Yii and Ykk and subtracted from Yik and Yki; admittances to earth (C/2 of the lines) are added to the diagonal. At node i:

Si* = Pi − jQi = Vi*·Σk YikVk
Pi = Σk |Vi||Vk||Yik| cos(θik − δi + δk)    Qi = −Σk |Vi||Vk||Yik| sin(θik − δi + δk)

The nodal method applied to power networks

The Ybus matrix is nothing other than the conductance matrix of § 1.2, written in complex numbers and with the earth as the reference node:

Ibus = Ybus·Vbus   ⇔   Vbus = Zbus·Ibus     Yii = Σj≠i yij + yi0    Yij = Yji = −yij

It is sparse (a node is connected to only a few neighbours) and symmetric: this is what makes networks of several thousand nodes computable. Zbus = Ybus−1, on the other hand, is full; its diagonal directly gives the Thévenin impedances seen from each node — those used for the short-circuit calculation (§ 2.7).

If the powers were replaced by impedances, the problem would become linear again and be solved in one step (this is NRJXpert's “linear network” calculation). It is the imposition of powers that forces iteration.

Line flows and losses

Once the voltages are known, the currents and powers at each end of a line are recovered:

Iij = yij(Vi − Vj) + yi0Vi     Sij = Vi·Iij*     Sji = Vj·Iji*     SL,ij = Sij + Sji

The sum Sij + Sji is not zero: it is the loss of the branch (positive for active power, and of either sign for reactive power depending on whether the line consumes or produces reactive power). The overall balance ΣPgen = ΣPloads + ΣPlosses is the first check of a load flow — it is the “network” version of the power balance of § 1.1.

📘2.6 Solution methods

Vi(n+1) = [ (Pi − jQi)/Vi(n)* − Σk≠i YikVk ] / Yii

Newton-Raphson

The power equations are linearised around the current state and solved for the corrections:

[ ΔP ; ΔQ ] = [ ∂P/∂δ   ∂P/∂|V| ; ∂Q/∂δ   ∂Q/∂|V| ] · [ Δδ ; Δ|V| ]    (Jacobian J, recalculated at each iteration)

Quadratic convergence: 3 to 6 iterations whatever the size of the network, but each iteration costs a factorisation of the Jacobian. It is the reference method.

Fast decoupled (Stott)

Assumptions: R ≪ X, small angles, |V| ≈ 1. Then ∂P/∂|V| ≈ 0 and ∂Q/∂δ ≈ 0, and the sub-matrices become constant (B' and B''): they are factorised only once. Very fast on transmission networks, but slow or divergent when R/X is large (distribution networks): see the lab below and the distribution load flow of level 5.

The Jacobian terms

Writing Ii = Σj|Yij||Vj|∠(θij + δj), we obtain the four Jacobian submatrices (off-diagonal terms, i ≠ j, and diagonal terms):

∂Pi/∂δj = −|Vi||Vj||Yij|·sin(θij − δi + δj)     ∂Pi/∂|Vj| = |Vi||Yij|·cos(θij − δi + δj)
∂Qi/∂δj = −|Vi||Vj||Yij|·cos(θij − δi + δj)     ∂Qi/∂|Vj| = −|Vi||Yij|·sin(θij − δi + δj)
∂Pi/∂|Vi| = 2|Vi||Yii|cos θii + Σj≠i|Vj||Yij|cos(θij − δi + δj)     ∂Qi/∂|Vi| = −2|Vi||Yii|sin θii − Σj≠i|Vj||Yij|sin(θij − δi + δj)

It was in these last two expressions (the diagonals) that the error corrected in the NRJXpert server in October 2026 was hiding: an index V[bs] instead of V[bus] made convergence go from 5 to 8 iterations.

Newton-Raphson algorithm

  1. Initialise (“flat” start: |V| = 1 pu, δ = 0, except for setpoints).
  2. Load buses: calculate Pi, Qi and the residuals ΔPi = Pisetpoint − Pi, ΔQi.
  3. Voltage-controlled buses (PV): calculate Pi and ΔPi only (|V| is imposed).
  4. Form the Jacobian and solve [ΔP; ΔQ] = J·[Δδ; Δ|V|].
  5. Update: δi ← δi + Δδi, |Vi| ← |Vi| + Δ|Vi|.
  6. Repeat until max(|ΔP|, |ΔQ|) < ε (typically 10⁻⁶ pu).

Gauss-Seidel, for its part, simply uses an acceleration factor applied to the PQ buses: Vi(k+1) = Vi(k) + α(Vi,calc − Vi(k)) with α = 1.3 to 1.7: simple to program, but its number of iterations grows with the size of the network, whereas that of Newton-Raphson is practically independent of it.

The synchronous machine in the network: E, Xd, X′d and X″d

The rotor of a generator, fed with direct current (the excitation), creates a field rotating at synchronous speed; the stator sees an EMF E proportional to the excitation. In steady state the machine is represented by this source E behind the synchronous reactance Xd:

E = V + jXd·I    ⇒    P = (E·V/Xd)·sin δ ,    Q = (E·V·cos δ − V²)/Xd

Active power is set by the turbine torque (internal angle δ), reactive power by the excitation: an over-excited machine (E cos δ > V) supplies reactive power and raises the voltage, an under-excited one absorbs it. This is why a generator is a PV bus in the load flow: its voltage regulator (AVR) adjusts E to hold V, and Q is a result of the calculation.

During a short circuit the rotor flux cannot change instantly: the apparent reactance of the machine goes from X″d (subtransient, first 10 to 50 ms), to X′d (transient, up to a few seconds) and then Xd (steady state). The lower the reactance, the larger the current: from 5–8 times rated current at the start to 1–2 times in steady state. Each study therefore uses the reactance that matches its time scale:

A synchronous motor is the same machine with negative active power: it consumes P but can still supply or absorb reactive power according to its excitation. Its short-circuit contribution is that of a generator.

Reactive power limits of generators

A PV node is held only if the generator can supply the necessary reactive power. The P-Q diagram (capability curve) of an alternator is bounded by the stator current (p² + q² ≤ v²), the field current (p² + (q + v²/Xd)² ≤ (v·Efd max/Xd)², rotor heating), the under-excitation limit (stability, absorbed reactive power) and the turbine power. When the requested reactive power exceeds a limit, the load flow sets Q at that limit: the node becomes PQ and its voltage is no longer held; it becomes PV again if the voltage comes back to the right side of the set-point (Capability, AVR, PSS… button of the generator dialog).

Operating region of a generator (v = 1 pu; Xd = 1.8; Efd max = 2.5). Parallel lines: zero-sequence mutual coupling

Two circuits on the same tower (or two cables in the same trench) are magnetically coupled. In the positive and negative sequences the three phases compensate each other and the coupling is negligible; in the zero sequence the three currents are in phase and return through the earth: the mutual impedance Z0m reaches 40 to 70 % of Z₀. For two parallel lines between the same nodes, with zero-sequence impedances ZA and ZB:

Z0,eq = (ZA·ZB − Z0m²) / (ZA + ZB − 2·Z0m)    = (Z0 + Z0m)/2 for two identical lines

The coupling therefore increases Z₀ seen from an earth fault and reduces its current; it also induces a zero-sequence current in the sound line, which distorts the measurement of distance protections (earth compensation factor). Induction machine: equivalent circuit, slip and torque

The rotor of an induction machine is not supplied: the rotating stator field (speed ns = 60·f/p) induces currents in it as soon as the rotor turns at a different speed n. The slip is defined as s = (ns − n)/ns: 1 at standstill, 2 to 4 % at rated load, negative for a generator (squirrel-cage wind turbine). Per phase, the machine is represented by Rs + jXs in series with the parallel combination of jXm (magnetisation) and Rr/s + jXr (rotor). The resistance Rr/s = Rr + Rr(1 − s)/s contains the mechanical power: Pmech = 3·Ir²·Rr·(1 − s)/s.

Two fundamental consequences for the network engineer:

  • At start (s = 1), Rr/s is small: the machine behaves as a low impedance and draws 5 to 7 times its rated current at a power factor of 0.2 to 0.3. This current causes the starting voltage dip.
  • Torque varies as the square of the voltage: neglecting the magnetising branch, T ≈ (3/ωs)·V²·(Rr/s)/[(Rs + Rr/s)² + (Xs + Xr)²]. At 80 % voltage the torque falls to 64 %. The slip at maximum torque does not depend on voltage; if the load torque exceeds the available maximum torque, the motor stalls: it slows down, its current stays high, which lowers the voltage even further.

Finally, an induction machine always consumes reactive power (magnetisation and leakage: 0.3 to 0.5 times its rated active power): in the load flow it is a PQ load whose slip (hence P and Q) NRJXpert computes for the bus voltage.

Left, per-phase equivalent circuit: Rs, Xs (stator), Xm (magnetising), Xr and Rr/s (rotor referred to the stator). At standstill (s = 1), Rr/s is small: high current (5 to 7 In). Right, torque-speed characteristic: torque varies as V² (the dotted curve is for a 20 % lower voltage). If the load torque exceeds the available torque, the motor cannot start or it stalls.

Contribution of induction motors to the short circuit

During the first cycles of a fault, the rotor flux of an induction motor maintains an emf: the motor feeds into the fault a current close to its starting current, which dies out within a few cycles. IEC 60909 represents it by the impedance ZM = Un²/(ILR/IrM · SrM) (positive and negative sequences; no zero-sequence path), with R/X = 0.10 (MV motors of at least 1 MW), 0.15 (MV under 1 MW) or 0.42 (LV, cables included); it increases I"k and ip but is neglected for the minimum current. Doubly-fed induction generator (DFIG)

Most 1.5 to 5 MW wind turbines installed between 2000 and 2020 are DFIGs: a wound-rotor induction machine whose stator is connected directly to the grid and whose rotor is supplied, through slip rings, by a back-to-back AC converter (rotor-side converter, DC link, grid-side converter) also connected to the grid. The converter injects currents of frequency s·f into the rotor: the machine can therefore turn at a speed different from synchronism (in practice 0.7 to 1.3 times the synchronous speed, i.e. s from +0.3 to −0.3) while delivering at grid frequency. The speed can follow the wind to extract maximum power (P ∝ v³, hence P ∝ ω³).

Wound-rotor induction machine: the stator is connected directly to the grid, the rotor through a back-to-back converter. The converter carries only the slip power Pr ≈ −s·Ps.

The great economic advantage is that the converter carries only the slip power Pr ≈ −s·Ps: 25 to 30 % of the rated power, against 100 % for a full-converter wind turbine (inverter model, level 5). The power delivered to the grid is P = Ps − Pr (with Pr the power absorbed by the rotor):

  • super-synchronous (s < 0, strong wind): the rotor delivers power to the grid in addition to the stator;
  • sub-synchronous (s > 0, light wind): the rotor absorbs power, taken from the grid by the converter;
  • synchronous (s = 0): the rotor is supplied with direct current, the converter carries only the losses.

The equivalent circuit is that of the induction machine (Rs, Xs, Xm, Rr, Xr) with, in addition, a rotor voltage Vr imposed by the converter: V = (Rs + jXs)·Is + jXm·(Is + Ir) and Vr = (Rr + js(Xr + Xm))·Ir + js·Xm·Is. In steady state the rotor-side converter controls the stator reactive power (Qs) and the torque, and the grid-side converter can supply or absorb additional reactive power within its apparent power, reduced by the slip active power it carries. The limits are therefore: rotor current (≈ 1.1 pu), stator current (≈ 1 pu), rotor voltage (proportional to s, hence more binding at high speed) and the rating of the grid-side converter.

In NRJXpert (type “Wind (DFIG)” of the generator bus, Distributed resources dialog): the load flow is the ordinary load flow (P, Q at the terminals); afterwards the program solves the equivalent circuit and displays slip, speed, Ps, Pr, Qs, the grid converter's Q, the currents Is, Ir and the voltage Vr, with a warning if a limit is exceeded. For a voltage-controlled generator (PVbus) the reactive limits of the load flow are deduced from the machine and its converters (PV ↔ PQ switching, as for a synchronous machine). The speed follows the maximum-power law, or the slip can be imposed.

DFIG and short circuit: the crowbar

During a deep voltage dip the machine's EMF drives very high rotor currents that would destroy the rotor-side converter. The latter is then blocked and the rotor is short-circuited by a crowbar (resistors): for a few tens of milliseconds the machine behaves like an ordinary induction machine, with a short-circuit current of 3 to 6 times rated current, much higher than that of a full-converter wind turbine (1.1 to 1.5 In). If the crowbar does not operate (shallow dip, or crowbar active for only a few cycles) the converter limits the current. NRJXpert offers both models: crowbar (impedance 1/Isc in positive and negative sequences, R/X = 0.1; no zero-sequence path) and converter limiting the current (current source Imax·In in the positive network, like any inverter). The DFIG contribution towards the end of the fault depends on its control: IEC 60909 refers to the manufacturer's values.

⚡2.8 Protection: what short-circuit currents are used for

  • Breaking capacity of the device ≥ maximum I"k (and ip ≤ making capacity) at the point of installation.
  • Sensitivity: the minimum short-circuit current (cmin, furthest fault, often phase-to-earth or phase-to-phase) must trip the protection within the required time: this is the « minimum Isc » calculation of NRJXpert.
  • Thermal withstand of cables: I²·t ≤ k²·S² (k ≈ 143 for XLPE-insulated copper).
  • Selectivity: only the protection immediately upstream of the fault must trip (current, time or logic selectivity).
  • Inverters: their contribution is limited to 1.1 to 1.5 times their rated current; a network supplied mainly by inverters has low fault currents, which makes detection harder (level 5).

The protection chain: measure, decide, interrupt

A protection is always a chain of three functions. The sensors (current transformers CT and voltage transformers VT) scale network quantities down to values that can be measured safely (1 or 5 A, 100 V). The relay compares these measurements with its settings and decides. The interrupting device carries out the order.

Protection chain: instrument transformers (CT, VT) scale current and voltage down to values the relay can read; the relay decides; the breaker interrupts. A fuse combines the three functions in a single device.
  • The circuit breaker interrupts any current up to its breaking capacity and can be closed again; the relay gives it the order.
  • The fuse combines sensor, relay and interruption: the element melts when the I²·t energy exceeds its pre-arcing value. At short-circuit currents it clears in less than half a cycle, before the peak (it “limits” the current), but must be replaced after each operation. Practical rule: two gG fuses in series are selective if the upstream rating is at least 1.6 times the downstream rating.
  • The recloser is a circuit breaker with a relay that closes again automatically: on an overhead line, 70 to 80 % of faults are transient (arc in air, tree branch) and disappear after a few hundred ms without voltage.

Why inverse-time curves?

The heating of a cable or transformer is proportional to I²·t: doubling the current divides the permissible time by four. An inverse-time protection follows this law: it tolerates a moderate overload for a long time (motor start, energisation) but trips quickly on a short circuit. The three families of IEC 60255-151 (SI, VI, EI) differ in steepness. The threshold Is fixes the asymptote, the TMS multiplier shifts the curve vertically. A fault current flows through every device in series between the source and the fault; for only the device closest to the fault to trip, its curve must lie below that of the upstream device, with at least 0.3 s between them. Two curves that cross are not selective.

Distance protection (21)

The relay divides voltage by current: Z = V/I. Since the impedance of a line is proportional to its length, Z measures the distance to the fault independently of current magnitude, which suits networks whose short-circuit power varies. Three zones (circular “mho” characteristics through the origin) are set on the line impedance: zone 1 at 80 % (instantaneous: 100 % is not used because of measurement and data errors, otherwise it would trip for a fault on the next line), zone 2 at 120 % with 0.3 s (covers the end of the line and acts as back-up), zone 3 at 200 % with 1 s. For an earth fault the loop impedance involves Z0; the relay corrects it with the factor k0 = (Z0 − Z1)/(3Z1), which the mutual coupling of a parallel line distorts (the relay then underestimates the distance: “under-reach”).

Differential protection (87)

It applies Kirchhoff's current law to the protected element (line, transformer, busbar): with no internal fault the sum of the incoming currents is zero; if it is not (allowing for a restraint proportional to through current, to cover CT errors) the fault is inside the zone and tripping is immediate. Selectivity is absolute, with no time delay: in return, a line needs a communication link between its two ends, and a transformer needs inrush restraint (not modelled in NRJXpert).

Common mistakes

  • Setting the threshold Is below the maximum load current: nuisance trips. It must sit above it (margin of 1.2 to 1.5) and well below the minimum short-circuit current (furthest fault, phase-to-phase or single-phase): otherwise the protection does not see the fault.
  • Checking selectivity with the maximum I″k only: trip time changes with current, so the curves must be examined at both extremes.
  • Forgetting CT saturation: beyond its accuracy limit factor it distorts the current and the relay trips too late (see below).
  • Relying on an ordinary earth-fault protection in an isolated or compensated network: the residual current is only a few amperes, a wattmetric or directional earth-fault protection is needed.
  • Confusing breaking capacity (the maximum current the device can interrupt) with the trip threshold (the current above which the relay orders the interruption).

Instrument transformers and surge arresters

A relay sees the network only through its current (CT) and voltage transformers (VT). Neutral earthing system: isolated, resistance, compensated or solid

The current of an earth fault depends mainly on how the network neutral is earthed. With an isolated neutral, it returns only through the zero-sequence capacitances of lines and cables: Ic = 3·ω·C0·Un/√3 (a few amperes to a few hundred amperes); operation can continue, but the sound phases rise to √3·V. A neutral resistor limits the current (typically 300 to 1 000 A in MV) while allowing a simple earth protection. The Petersen coil compensates the capacitive current: tuned (ω·L = 1/(3·ω·C0)), it leaves only a small residual current ≈ Ic·√(v² + r²) (detuning v, active current r of the parallel resistor), which extinguishes most transient faults; detection then requires wattmetric protections. With a solidly earthed neutral, the current is high and the overvoltages low (k ≤ 1.4).

The single-phase fault can only close through the capacitances C0 of the other two phases (current Ic, a few amperes to a few hundred amperes) and through the neutral impedance Zn. With an isolated neutral there is only Ic; with a Petersen coil IL opposes Ic: the two currents subtract and only a residual current of a few amperes remains, which extinguishes most transient faults. In every system except solid earthing the healthy phases rise to √3 times the phase voltage.

Earth-fault protection in isolated or compensated networks

In a network earthed through a resistor or solidly, an earth fault drives a large current in the faulted feeder: an earth overcurrent protection (50N/51N) is enough. With an isolated or compensated neutral the fault current is only a few amperes to a few tens of amperes, the same order as the capacitive current Ic of each healthy feeder: a current threshold can no longer tell the faulted feeder from the others. One must look at the direction of the zero-sequence power.

The fault is the only “generator” of the zero-sequence network: the residual voltage V0 appears on the whole busbar and every feeder sees the same V0. Each healthy feeder absorbs its own capacitive current 3·I0 = 3·V0·jC0·ω, 90° ahead of V0; the faulted feeder sends back toward the busbar the sum of the capacitive currents of the other feeders (and, with a Petersen coil, the coil current). The zero-sequence power S0 = 3·V0·I0* seen from the line side therefore has a different sign in the faulted feeder and in the healthy ones:

  • Isolated network: varmetric protection (−90° characteristic). The faulted feeder has a negative (capacitive) reactive power Q0, the healthy feeders a positive one.
  • Compensated network: wattmetric protection (0° characteristic). The coil cancels almost all the reactive power, so it no longer tells anything; what remains is the active power P0, which comes from the losses of the coil and of the parallel resistor. It is positive and large in the faulted feeder, almost zero in the healthy ones.

The protection trips if the residual voltage exceeds a threshold (typically 10 to 30 % of the phase voltage, so as not to react to the normal unbalance), if the power projected on the characteristic exceeds its threshold, and after its time delay. In NRJXpert: protection type “Directional earth fault 67N / wattmetric 32N” of a switching device; the short-circuit summary gives V0, P0 and Q0 seen by each device.

Transformer differential protection (87T)

A transformer is an expensive device whose internal faults (turn-to-turn, winding-to-winding, bushing failure) must be cleared within a few tens of milliseconds. The differential protection compares the currents entering and leaving the zone delimited by its two CTs: without an internal fault the primary and secondary ampere-turns balance and the differential current Id = |I1 + I2| is zero. But three effects make it more delicate than a line differential:

  • the transformation ratio and the phase shift of the vector group (Dyn11, YNd1…): the relay corrects the currents (CT ratios, software vector-group compensation, filtering of the zero-sequence current on the star side);
  • the tap position: an on-load tap changer changes the ratio by ± 10 % without the relay knowing it, hence a differential current of ± 10 % of the through current even without a fault;
  • CT saturation and the magnetising inrush at energisation (up to 10 times rated current, rich in 2nd harmonic), which create a differential current without any fault.

The remedy is the percentage restraint: the operating threshold grows with the through current (the bias Ib = (|I1| + |I2|)/2): the relay trips if Id > max(Id,min, k1·Ib), with a slope k1 of 20 to 30 % and a second slope k2 (50 to 70 %) beyond a break point to account for CT saturation at high currents. An unrestrained instantaneous threshold Iu (8 to 10 times In) clears large internal faults at once, even if a CT saturates. 2nd-harmonic restraint blocks tripping at energisation.

In NRJXpert (type “Transformer differential 87T”): the vector group is assumed compensated and the zero-sequence current filtered. The differential current comes from the ratio mismatch (tap ≠ ratio the relay is set for) and from the saturation of the input-side CT; an option “fault inside the zone” places the fault between the transformer and its CT. Magnetising inrush is not simulated.

Limits of the NRJXpert models

A model simplifies: you must know what it does not represent so as not to make it say what it cannot say.

  • Mutual coupling: zero sequence only; taken into account in the short circuit, not in the distribution sweep.
  • Induction machine: a single rotor cage. DFIG: steady-state model (equivalent circuit, lossless converters); the dynamic simulation represents it by an inverter (current source with ride-through), without rotor-flux, crowbar or vector-control dynamics; the crowbar is a choice of the short-circuit model, not a time-based logic; no double-cage machine.
  • Generator: the capability curve acts in the classic load flow; the PSS tuning must be checked for each configuration.
  • Protection: it does not trip in dynamic simulations; transformer differential without magnetising inrush nor 2nd-harmonic restraint; time grading only; CT saturation by simple clipping.
  • Surge arresters: coordination by a static formula (Upl + 2·S·L/v); lightning and switching overvoltages are not simulated.
  • Neutral earthing: steady state (no arc transient); the zero-sequence capacitance C0 is that of the whole network, which is wrong if transformers separate several zones.

💰2.9 Economic dispatch of generation

The load flow answers the question “what becomes of the network if the units produce this?” The next question is “how much should each unit produce?”: fuel costs differ from one plant to another, and the installed capacity exceeds the demand plus losses. There is therefore an infinite number of possible dispatches, of which only one is the cheapest.

The simplest case: no losses, no limits

The hourly cost of a thermal unit is well fitted by a parabola: Ci = αi + βiPi + γiPi² (F/h or $/h). We minimise Ct = ΣCi subject to the constraint ΣPi = PD. The Lagrangian L = Ct + λ(PD − ΣPi) and the conditions ∂L/∂Pi = 0 give:

d Ci/d Pi = βi + 2γiPi = λ   (for all units)   ⇒   Pi = (λ − βi)/(2γi)     λ = [PD + Σ βi/(2γi)] / Σ 1/(2γi)

Equal incremental cost rule: at the optimum, the cost of the last MWh produced is the same everywhere. λ is this common marginal cost, expressed in F/MWh: it is the system price, the one used as a reference by electricity markets.

Economic dispatch: λ is raised until the sum of the powers read on the marginal-cost lines equals the demand. The unit whose line is flattest (low γ) produces the most.

With unit limits

Adding Pi,min ≤ Pi ≤ Pi,max, the Kuhn-Tucker conditions give the same rule, except for units stuck at a limit:

d Ci/d Pi = λ if Pi,min < Pi < Pi,max    ≤ λ if Pi = Pi,max    ≥ λ if Pi = Pi,min

We solve by iterating on λ (Newton's method): at each step, the Pi are calculated, clipped at the limits, and λ is corrected by Δλ = ΔP/Σ(1/2γi) over the free units only, until ΣPi = PD.

Accounting for network losses

Losses depend on the dispatch: generating far from the loads costs losses. They are expressed by Kron's loss coefficients (Bij, calculated from a reference load flow):

PL = ΣiΣj PiBijPj + Σi B0iPi + B00     constraint: Σ Pi = PD + PL

The optimality condition becomes dCi/dPi + λ·∂PL/∂Pi = λ, that is

(d Ci/d Pi)·Li = λ    with the penalty factor Li = 1/(1 − ∂PL/∂Pi)    ⇒    Pi = [λ(1 − B0i) − βi − 2λΣj≠iBijPj] / [2(γi + λBii)]
  1. Initialise λ.
  2. Calculate the Pi (formula above, clipped at the limits) and the losses PL.
  3. Calculate ΔP = PD + PL − ΣPi, then Δλ = ΔP/Σ(∂Pi/∂λ).
  4. λ ← λ + Δλ; return to step 2 until |ΔP| < ε.

Common simplified case (Bij = 0, B₀₀ = 0): PL = ΣBiiPi² and Pi = (λ − βi)/[2(γi + λBii)].

This “single-bus” formulation ignores the network (voltage constraints, thermal limits of lines), the minimum up and down times of the units, storage and renewables. Level 5 gives the modern version: multi-period OPF and unit commitment formulated as mixed-integer linear programming (MILP), with load-flow control — this is what the server's der/ package calculates (economics/dispatch.py, economics/opf.py). The equal incremental cost rule remains its core.

  1. Estimate the position needed to bring the load voltage back to at least 0.98 pu. Check with the load flow with a fixed tap, for positions −2, −3, −4 and −5.
  2. Switch to automatic control (regulated voltage: secondary side of the transformer, setpoint 1.00 pu, dead band 2 %). What does the calculation choose?
  3. Reduce the dead band to 0.5 %. What does the calculation report, and why?
Show the solution

1. Each position raises the voltage by about 1.25 % (slightly more at the load bus, because the current decreases): from 0.922 to 0.98 pu takes a little more than 4 positions. Load flow: 0.950; 0.965; 0.979; 0.995 pu for −2; −3; −4; −5. Position −4 is just below 0.98 pu: −5 is needed.

2. The band is [0.99; 1.01] pu: the calculation stops at position −5 (0.995 pu); −4 gives 0.979 pu, below the band.

3. A hunting warning: one position changes the voltage by 1.5 %, more than the width of the band (0.5 %); no position falls inside the band and the calculation oscillates between two positions. The dead band must be wider than the effect of one position.

Exercise 10 — Inverse-time curves

An overcurrent relay has a threshold Is = 200 A and TMS = 0.2. Compute its trip time (excluding breaker opening time) for a current of 1,000 A, then 2,000 A, with the SI (k = 0.14, α = 0.02), VI (13.5 and 1) and EI (80 and 2) curves. Which curve should you choose for a line where the fault current varies widely with fault location?

See the solution

t = TMS·k/((I/Is)α − 1). At 1,000 A (I/Is = 5): SI 0.856 s, VI 0.675 s, EI 0.667 s. At 2,000 A (ratio 10): SI 0.594 s, VI 0.300 s, EI 0.162 s. The SI curve is almost flat (0.86 s → 0.59 s); the EI curve collapses when the current doubles (0.67 s → 0.16 s). It therefore suits cases where the fault current varies widely with position (fast for a close fault, slow for a remote one, which makes grading easier) and where the protection must tolerate load energisation (downstream fuses); SI suits cases where the fault current varies little (strong network).

Exercise 11 — Compensated network

A 20 kV, 50 Hz network has a total zero-sequence capacitance C0 = 8 µF. Compute the capacitive current Ic of a single-phase fault with isolated neutral and the voltage of the healthy phases. Compute the inductance of the tuned Petersen coil, then with a detuning v = +5 % and an active current of 3 % of Ic, and the residual fault current.

See the solution

Ic = 3·ω·C0·Un/√3 = 3 × 314.16 × 8·10⁻⁶ × 20,000/√3 = 87.1 A; the healthy phases rise to √3 × 11.55 kV = 20 kV (line-to-line voltage). Tuned coil: L = 1/(3·ω²·C0) = 0.422 H. With v = +5 % (over-compensation): L = 1/(3·ω²·C0·1.05) = 0.402 H; residual current ≈ Ic·√(v² + r²) = 87.1 × √(0.05² + 0.03²) = 5.1 A, 17 times less than with isolated neutral: the arc of a transient fault extinguishes by itself. Detuning avoids exact resonance (which would make the neutral voltage rise sharply); exact tuning is done by varying L until the neutral voltage peaks.

Exercise 12 — Current transformer saturation

A 200/5 A, class 5P20, 15 VA CT has a secondary resistance Rct = 0.3 Ω. It feeds a relay and leads with a total burden of 30 VA (at 5 A). Compute its actual accuracy limit factor ALF′. Does the CT remain accurate for a 3 kA fault? What should be done?

See the solution

ALF′ = ALF·(Rct·Isn² + Sn)/(Rct·Isn² + Sb) = 20 × (0.3 × 25 + 15)/(0.3 × 25 + 30) = 20 × 22.5/37.5 = 12. The CT keeps its composite error up to 12 × 200 = 2,400 A. A 3,000 A fault, 15 times the rated current, exceeds this limit: the CT saturates, the relay sees a lower current and trips later. Solutions: reduce the burden (larger lead cross-section, low-burden relay) to restore ALF′ ≥ 15, or choose a CT with a higher rated burden (30 VA) or a higher ratio.

Exercise 13 — Motor start and voltage dip

A 2 MVA motor starts direct-on-line with a starting current of 6 times its rated current, on a network with a short-circuit power of 40 MVA. Its starting torque is 1.2 pu (at rated voltage) and the pump it drives opposes a load torque of 0.5 pu at standstill. Estimate the voltage dip, then the accelerating torque at start. Does the motor start? What happens if the network is only 25 MVA?

See the solution

Power drawn at start: Sd = 6 × 2 = 12 MVA; dip ≈ Sd/Ssc = 12/40 = 30 % (first order: refine it with a load flow, see example 5.5). Voltage falls to 0.70 pu; torque is 1.2 × 0.70² = 0.59 pu, an accelerating torque of 0.59 − 0.50 = 0.09 pu: the motor starts, but very slowly (current stays high for a long time and heats the motor). With 25 MVA: dip ≈ 48 %, voltage 0.52 pu, torque 1.2 × 0.52² = 0.32 pu < 0.50: the motor does not start. Remedies: star-delta starter, soft starter, variable-speed drive, network reinforcement or reactive compensation.

Exercise 14 — Generator capability curve

A 10 MVA generator (Xd = 1.8 pu, maximum excitation Efd max = 2.5 pu) produces 8 MW. For a voltage of 1.00 pu and then 0.95 pu, compute the maximum reactive power it can supply, with the stator limit p² + q² ≤ v² and the excitation limit p² + (q + v²/Xd)² ≤ (v·Efd max/Xd)². Which limit is reached first?

See the solution

p = 0.8 pu. At v = 1.00: stator q ≤ √(1 − 0.64) = 0.600 pu (6.0 Mvar); excitation q ≤ −1/1.8 + √((2.5/1.8)² − 0.64) = −0.556 + 1.136 = 0.580 pu (5.80 Mvar): excitation is the limit. At v = 0.95: stator q ≤ √(0.9025 − 0.64) = 0.512 pu (5.12 Mvar); excitation q ≤ −0.9025/1.8 + √((0.95 × 2.5/1.8)² − 0.64) = 0.548 pu: now the stator current is the limit. A low voltage therefore reduces reactive capability just when the network needs it most; in the load flow, the PV bus becomes PQ as soon as the requested reactive power exceeds this limit and the voltage is no longer held (example 2.14).

Exercise 15 — Distance protection of a line

A 10 km line has an impedance of 0.3 + j0.4 Ω/km. A distance relay is set with the standard zones (80 % instantaneous, 120 % at 0.3 s, 200 % at 1 s). Compute the reaches in ohms. Which zone sees a solid fault at 6 km, 9 km, 11 km (on the next line)? What is the effect, on an earth fault, of a parallel line coupled in zero sequence?

See the solution

Zline = 10 × (0.3 + j0.4) = 3 + j4 Ω, |Z| = 5 Ω. Reaches: zone 1 = 4 Ω, zone 2 = 6 Ω, zone 3 = 10 Ω. Fault at 6 km: Z = 3 Ω < 4 Ω, zone 1, instantaneous trip. At 9 km: 4.5 Ω, between 4 and 6 Ω, zone 2, trip at 0.3 s (the end of the line is not cleared instantly; a teleprotection scheme or a differential protection would fill this gap). At 11 km (beyond the line): 5.5 Ω, seen in zone 2: the relay is then back-up for the next line, delayed by 0.3 s: its own protection must act first. A parallel line coupled in zero sequence drives an extra residual current in the protected line which distorts the earth-fault impedance measurement: the relay under- or over-estimates the distance depending on current direction, which requires reducing zone 1 (70 % instead of 80 %) or compensating with the parallel line current.

Exercise 16 — Mutual coupling of two parallel lines

Two identical lines connect a source substation to a 20 kV delivery substation in parallel (Un = 20 kV, c = 1.1). Each has a zero-sequence impedance Z0 = 6 + j18 Ω and a zero-sequence mutual impedance Z0m = 2 + j8 Ω (currents in the same direction). Seen from the delivery substation, the positive-sequence impedance (upstream network and the lines in parallel) is Z1 = Z2 = 2 + j6 Ω. Calculate the single-phase earth-fault current I″k1 at the delivery substation, without then with the coupling. Does the three-phase fault change?

Show solution

Without coupling, the two lines in parallel give Z0/2 = 3 + j9 Ω; Z1 + Z2 + Z0 = 7 + j21 Ω, modulus 22.1 Ω: I″k1 = √3 × 1.1 × 20,000/22.1 = 1,721 A. With coupling: Z0,eq = (Z0 + Z0m)/2 = 4 + j13 Ω; sum 8 + j25 Ω, modulus 26.2 Ω: I″k1 = 1,452 A, i.e. −16 %. The three-phase fault, I″k3 = 1.1 × 20,000/(√3 × 6.32) = 2,008 A, does not change: in positive sequence the three currents cancel and the mutual has no effect. Practical consequence: a distance relay that neglects the mutual misjudges the distance of an earth fault, hence the earth compensation factor.

Exercise 17 — Choosing and placing a surge arrester

A 20 kV network (Um = 24 kV) supplies a transformer with a lightning impulse withstand BIL = 125 kV. The chosen arrester has a residual voltage Upl = 65 kV; the lightning wave has a steepness S = 1,000 kV/µs and a margin of 1.2 on the BIL is required. (1) What is the maximum distance between arrester and transformer? (2) Which rated voltage Ur should be chosen (among 18, 21, 24, 27 kV, with a temporary overvoltage withstand factor T = 1.05) with an isolated neutral (k = √3), then with a solidly earthed neutral (k = 1.4)?

Show solution

(1) The voltage at the transformer is Ueq = Upl + 2·S·L/v (v = 300 m/µs) and must stay ≤ 125/1.2 = 104.2 kV. So 2 × 1,000 × L/300 ≤ 39.2, i.e. L ≤ 5.9 m: the arrester must be as close as possible to the transformer. (2) Temporary overvoltage k·Um/√3. Isolated neutral: √3 × 24/√3 = 24 kV, so Ur ≥ 24/1.05 = 22.9 kV: 24 kV. Solidly earthed: 1.4 × 24/√3 = 19.4 kV, so Ur ≥ 18.5 kV: 21 kV (18 kV is not enough). An arrester with a higher rated voltage has a higher residual voltage: re-check the margin of question (1) with the Upl of the chosen model.

  3. so that time is the same whatever the current

📘Introduction

Objectives

  • Calculate currents, voltages and power in a DC or single-phase AC circuit.
  • Solve a network by mesh currents, node potentials, Millman, superposition, Thévenin, Norton and Kennelly.
  • Handle phasors and complex impedances; build the power triangle.
  • Size a power-factor correction capacitor bank and recognise a resonance risk.
  • Move from a simple quantity to a composed quantity in three-phase systems; calculate a star or delta load, balanced or not, and measure its power.
  • Decompose an unbalanced system into positive, negative and zero-sequence components.
  • Draw a circuit in NRJXpert, run the calculation and read the results.

Prerequisites: complex-number algebra (rectangular and polar form), trigonometry. Duration: 14 to 18 h.

🔹1.1 Basic quantities and laws

Voltage, current, power

Voltage U (volt, V) is a potential difference between two points; current I (ampere, A) is the flow of charge in a conductor. A two-terminal element receives the power P = U·I (watt, W) in the receiver convention (arrows of U and I in opposite directions); if it supplies power (generator), the generator convention is used. Energy is W = P·t (joule; in practice Wh or kWh: 1 kWh = 3.6 MJ).

Ohm's law and combinations

U = R·I    P = R·I² = U²/R
Series: Req = R₁ + R₂ + …    Parallel: 1/Req = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + … (two resistors: R₁R₂/(R₁ + R₂))

Voltage divider (series): U₂ = E·R₂/(R₁ + R₂). Current divider (parallel): I₂ = I·R₁/(R₁ + R₂).

The resistance of a conductor of length L and cross-section S is R = ρ·L/S; for copper ρ ≈ 0.0175 Ω·mm²/m at 20 °C and 0.0225 Ω·mm²/m at the service temperature used by NF C 15-100. This formula is used everywhere when selecting cables (level 3).

Kirchhoff's laws

Node law: Σ incoming currents = Σ outgoing currents    Mesh law: Σ voltages around a mesh = 0

For a node connected to several branches (Ek, Rk), Millman's theorem gives its voltage directly: V = Σ(Ek/Rk) / Σ(1/Rk). It is the single-node special case of the node-potential method used by the NRJXpert server: the node law is written at each node, which gives a linear system Y·V = J (admittance matrix Y, potentials V, injected currents J).

Example 1.1 — Series and parallel circuit

A battery E = 12 V supplies R₁ = 4 Ω and R₂ = 8 Ω. Calculate currents, voltages and power (a) in series, (b) in parallel.

Show solution

(a) Req = 12 Ω; I = 12/12 = 1 A; U₁ = 4 V, U₂ = 8 V (voltage divider: 12 × 8/12); P = 12 W (4 W + 8 W).

(b) Req = 4 × 8/12 = 2.667 Ω; I = 4.5 A; I₁ = 3 A, I₂ = 1.5 A; P = 54 W. The smallest resistance takes the most current.

Example 1.2 — Two sources in parallel (Millman)

A source E₁ = 12 V with internal resistance R₁ = 2 Ω and a source E₂ = 9 V, R₂ = 3 Ω supply a load R₃ = 6 Ω together. Calculate the load voltage and the current of each source.

Show solution

V = (12/2 + 9/3 + 0/6)/(1/2 + 1/3 + 1/6) = 9/1 = 9 V. I₃ = 9/6 = 1.5 A; I₁ = (12 − 9)/2 = 1.5 A; I₂ = (9 − 9)/3 = 0 A. The 9 V source delivers nothing: its EMF equals the node voltage. With E₂ = 8 V it would absorb current (it would be recharged): this is what happens to a weak battery connected in parallel with a charged one.

Sign conventions

Before any calculation the arrows must be oriented. The current iab is counted positive from a to b; the voltage uab is the potential difference va − vb. Two conventions coexist:

ConventionArrows of u and iPower receivedUse
Receiveropposite directionsp = u·iresistors, motors, loads
Generatorsame directionp = −u·isources, alternators

In a generator the current flows towards rising potentials, in a receiver towards falling potentials. A negative result is never an error: it simply means that the real quantity is in the opposite direction to the chosen arrow — a source crossed “backwards” behaves as a receiver (it is recharged). NRJXpert displays each current and each voltage in the component's own direction; this is why a current may appear negative (or 180° out of phase).

Sign conventions. The arrows of u and i are opposite in the receiver convention, in the same direction in the generator convention; p is the power received by the two-terminal element.

Active two-terminal element: the two models of a source

An active two-terminal element is an energy generator. Its output voltage depends on the current delivered:

U = E − Ri·I    (E: electromotive force, open-circuit voltage; Ri: internal resistance)

Dividing this relation by Ri gives E/Ri = U/Ri + I: the same source can also be written as a current source J = E/Ri = Isc in parallel with Ri. The two models are equivalent seen from outside (but not for the balance of internal losses).

Voltage model: E, Ri in series   ⇔   Current model: J = E/Ri, Ri in parallel

A source is ideal when Ri → 0 (voltage source: U = E whatever the load) or Ri → ∞ (current source: I = J whatever the load). A source is called dependent (or controlled) when its value depends on a voltage or a current of the circuit: this is the case of the small-signal equivalent circuits of transistors (h parameters) and, in power networks, of inverter models. NRJXpert handles independent sources, DC and AC, ideal or with internal impedance; the inverter-based generators of level 5 are, in short circuit, current sources.

The two models of the same source: J = E/Ri = Isc. They are equivalent seen from terminals 1-2, but not for internal losses (Thévenin ↔ Norton).

Generalised Ohm's law

For any branch made of resistances and EMFs in series, carrying the current Iab:

Uab = Va − Vb = Rab·Iab − Eab    with Rab = Σ Ri and Eab = Σ ±Ei
Generalised Ohm's law: Uab = Rab·Iab − Eab, with Rab = R₁ + R₂ and Eab = E₁ − E₂ (“+” sign for an EMF oriented from a to b).

Sign of the EMFs: “+” if Ei is oriented from a to b (in the chosen current direction), “−” otherwise. This single relation replaces mesh-by-mesh writing; it is what allows, once the node potentials are known, to return to the branch currents (§ 1.2).

Power balance

A network calculation is only finished once it has been checked that all the power produced is consumed:

Pgen = Σ ±Ei·Ii + Σ ±Ui·Ji = Preceived = Σ Ri·Ii²

Each term Ei·Ii (or Ui·Ji for a current source) takes the “+” sign if Ei and Ii are in the same direction (the source delivers), the “−” sign otherwise (the source receives). This is the safest check of a hand calculation as well as of an NRJXpert calculation: read the currents of all the branches and check that the equality holds to within 1 %.

Example 1.3 — A source seen through its two models

A battery of EMF E = 12 V and internal resistance Ri = 0.2 Ω delivers into a 2 Ω load. Calculate I and U with the two models, then the efficiency of the source.

Show solution

Voltage model: I = 12/(0.2 + 2) = 5.455 A; U = 12 − 0.2 × 5.455 = 10.91 V.

Current model: J = 12/0.2 = 60 A in parallel with 0.2 Ω; current divider: I = 60 × 0.2/(0.2 + 2) = 5.455 A. Identical.

Balance: Pgen = 12 × 5.455 = 65.5 W; load 2 × 5.455² = 59.5 W; internal losses 0.2 × 5.455² = 5.95 W; efficiency 90.9 %. In short circuit, Isc = 60 A: this is the short-circuit current of the source, a notion that will be met again in networks (level 2) in the form I″k = c·Un/(√3·Zk).

🧮1.2 Methods for solving linear circuits

Kirchhoff's laws are enough to write the equations of a network, but they give many of them: b unknown currents for b branches. The methods that follow reduce the number of equations and can be programmed; they are the ones used by the NRJXpert calculation server. They are presented in DC, but apply unchanged in AC by replacing R by Z and G by Y (§ 1.3).

Mesh-current method

Choose b − n + 1 independent meshes and assign a fictitious mesh current to each. The real current of a branch is the algebraic sum of the currents of the meshes it belongs to; a branch that belongs to only one mesh carries the current of that mesh. The system is written directly:

[R]·[I] = [E]   ⇒   [I] = [R]−1·[E]
R11·II + R12·III + … + R1N·IN = EI    …
TermRule
Rii — self-resistancesum of all the resistances of mesh i (always positive)
Rij = Rji — mutual resistancesum of the resistances common to meshes i and j, given a “−” sign if the two mesh currents flow through the common branch in opposite directions
Ei — mesh EMFalgebraic sum of the EMFs of the mesh: “+” if the EMF is oriented in the chosen direction of travel

Node-potential method

One node is chosen as the potential reference (V = 0) — the potential difference between two points does not depend on this choice — and the node law is written at the other n − 1 nodes, expressing each current by the generalised Ohm's law. This gives a linear system whose unknowns are the potentials:

[G]·[V] = [J]   ⇒   [V] = [G]−1·[J]
Gaa·Va − Gab·Vb − … − Gan·Vn = Ja    …
TermRule
Gaa — self-conductancesum of the conductances of all the branches ending at node a
Gab = Gba — mutual conductancesum of the conductances of the branches directly connecting a and b (preceded by the “−” sign in the system)
Ja — “node current”sum of the E·G and J terms of the branches ending at the node, “+” if the source is oriented towards the node

Once the potentials are obtained, the currents follow from the generalised Ohm's law: Ik = (Va − Vb + Ek)·Gk. This is the method of the NRJXpert server (admittance matrix Y·V = J, extended by modified nodal analysis to zero-impedance branches: ideal voltage source, wire, ammeter, inductor in DC).

Applied to a two-node network, it reduces to Millman's theorem:

UO′O = (Σ ±Ek·Gk + Σ ±Jk) / Σ Gk

Superposition theorem

In a linear network, the current of a branch is the sum of the currents that each source would produce there acting alone, the other sources being replaced by their internal resistance (short circuit for an ideal voltage source, open circuit for an ideal current source). Useful when the sources are of different natures (DC + AC, several frequencies): each regime is calculated separately, then superposed. Beware: powers do not superpose (P = RI² is not linear).

Equivalent generator: Thévenin and Norton

To find only the current of a single branch, that branch is disconnected and the whole rest of the network (seen from the two terminals 1-2) is replaced by a single source:

Thévenin: Eth = U12 open-circuit, Rth = Req,12   ⇒   I = Eth/(Rth + R)
Norton: JN = I12 short-circuit, RN = Rth   ⇒   I = JN·RN/(RN + R)

Req,12 is calculated by switching the sources off (EMFs short-circuited, current sources opened) and reducing the passive network. These two theorems are the basis of short-circuit calculation: at the fault point, the whole network is a Thévenin generator of EMF c·Un/√3 and impedance Zk (level 2).

Example 1.5 — Thévenin and Norton on the same branch

E₁ = 36 V in series with R₁ = 12 Ω; a current source I02 = 6 A in parallel with R₂ = 6 Ω; the whole supplies R = 5 Ω. Calculate the current in R.

Example 1.5: E₁ = 36 V, R₁ = 12 Ω, I₀₂ = 6 A, R₂ = 6 Ω, R = 5 Ω. The network to the left of terminals 1-2 is replaced by Eth = 36 V in series with Rth = 4 Ω (Thévenin), or by JN = 9 A in parallel with 4 Ω (Norton).
Show solution

Thévenin. Open-circuit (branch R disconnected), Millman: Eth = (E₁G₁ + I02)/(G₁ + G₂) = (3 + 6)/(1/12 + 1/6) = 36 V. Sources switched off: Rth = R₁∥R₂ = 12 × 6/18 = 4 Ω. Hence IR = 36/(4 + 5) = 4 A.

Norton. Terminals 1-2 short-circuited: JN = E₁/R₁ + I02 = 3 + 6 = 9 A; RN = 4 Ω. Current divider: IR = 9 × 4/(4 + 5) = 4 A. The two models are indeed equivalent (Eth = RN·JN).

Kennelly's theorem: star ↔ delta transformation

Three impedances in star and three impedances in delta are equivalent seen from their three terminals a, b, c:

Star → delta: Rab = Ra + Rb + RaRb/Rc    Rbc = Rb + Rc + RbRc/Ra    Rca = Rc + Ra + RcRa/Rb
Delta → star: Ra = RabRca/(Rab+Rbc+Rca)    (likewise for Rb, Rc)
Kennelly's theorem: the two connections are equivalent seen from a, b and c. Balanced case: RΔ = 3·RY.

Balanced case: RΔ = 3·RY. This is the key to three-phase load calculation (§ 1.6: a delta load absorbs three times the power of the same load in star) and to the NRJXpert single-line diagram, which reduces any delta load to its equivalent star (R/3, L/3, 3C). It is also used to reduce a bridge (Wheatstone circuit) that neither series nor parallel combination can simplify.

Practical work — The two methods checked with NRJXpert

  2. Calculation Linear network, method Node potentials, F5.
  3. Compare the six currents with the hand calculation: signs included. The zero current of R₂ is a good test of the orientation of the sources.
  4. Check the balance: read I and U of each component in the results table, calculate ΣR·I² and ΣE·I (18 W).
  5. Disconnect branch R₄ (delete the link) and read the open-circuit voltage between its two terminals with a voltmeter: this is Eth. Reconnect R₄ and check I₄ = Eth/(Rth + R₄).

The software knows neither meshes nor Thévenin: it solves Y·V = J. These methods remain essential to predict the order of magnitude and to check the result.

🔸1.3 Sinusoidal steady state

Sinusoidal quantities and phasors

A voltage u(t) = U√2·sin(ωt + φu) is characterised by its RMS value U, its angular frequency ω = 2πf (314.16 rad/s at 50 Hz) and its phase φu. It is represented by the phasor (complex number) U = U·ejφu = U∠φu. All the quantities of a linear circuit have the same frequency: phasors turn differential equations into complex algebraic equations, and Ohm's and Kirchhoff's laws apply unchanged.

A 230 V, 50 Hz source supplies a 10 Ω resistor through a line R = 0.5 Ω, L = 10 mH. Calculate the current, the load voltage and the voltage drop in the line.

Show solution

Average value, RMS value

A signal is alternating if its instantaneous value takes successively positive and negative values, periodic if it repeats identically every T seconds. Two quantities characterise it:

Uavg = (1/T)·∫₀T u(t)·dt      I = √( (1/T)·∫₀T i²(t)·dt )    (RMS value)

The RMS value is defined by its effects: it is the value of the direct current that would release the same amount of heat in a resistor during one period (Q = ∫R·i²dt = R·I²T). For a sinusoid, I = Im/√2 and Uavg = 0. All measuring instruments and all rating plates give RMS values: “230 V” means Um = 325 V.

From rotating vectors to phasors

A vector of length Im rotating at speed ω has the projection Im·sin(ωt + Ψ): to every sinusoidal quantity corresponds a rotating vector, and conversely. Hence Fresnel's rule: the sum of several sinusoidal quantities of the same frequency is a sinusoidal quantity of the same frequency, the geometric resultant of the vectors — it is much easier to add vectors than functions of time.

The cissoidal transformation formalises this step: to i(t) = Im·sin(ωt + Ψi) we associate the complex quantity I(t) = Im·ej(ωt+Ψi), whose imaginary part is the real signal. Since all the quantities of a network rotate together, the factor ejωt cancels: it is enough to keep the position of the vectors at t = 0, which is the phasor:

Σ Ik = 0 (node)      Σ Ek = Σ Zk·Ik (mesh)

Kirchhoff's laws therefore apply to phasors, and not to RMS values: three RMS voltages of 100 V, 150 V and 200 V can perfectly well coexist in the same mesh (exercise 6). ⚡1.4 Power and reactive power compensation

S = U·I* = P + jQ    P = U·I·cos φ (W)    Q = U·I·sin φ (var)    S = U·I = √(P² + Q²) (VA)    cos φ = P/S

Active power P produces work (heat, motion); reactive power Q only makes energy “oscillate” between the source and the magnetic fields (motors, transformers) or electric fields (capacitors). It is not billed at low voltage, but it increases the current for the same active power: more losses (R·I²), more voltage drop and larger cable cross-sections. Hence compensation: a capacitor in parallel supplies the reactive power that the load absorbs.

QC = P·(tan φ₁ − tan φ₂)    C = QC/(ω·U²) (single-phase)    three-phase, capacitors in delta: C = QC/(3ω·U²)

From instantaneous power to the three powers

With u = Umsin(ωt + Ψu) and i = Imsin(ωt + Ψi), the instantaneous power p = u·i oscillates at 2ω around an average value: this average is the active power.

P = (1/T)·∫₀T p(t)·dt = U·I·cos φ = R·I² (W)     Q = U·I·sin φ = ±X·I² (var)     S = U·I = √(P² + Q²) (VA)

The sign of Q follows the nature of the two-terminal element: “+” for an inductive element (it consumes reactive power), “−” for a capacitive one (it produces reactive power). The four similar triangles — currents (Ia, Ir), voltages (Ua, Ur), impedances (R, X) and powers (P, Q) — are deduced from one another by multiplying by I, I and I: the same angle φ appears in each.

Boucherot's theorem. Active powers add up among themselves, reactive powers among themselves (with their sign); apparent powers do not add up: P = ΣPk, Q = ΣQk, S = √(P² + Q²) ≤ ΣSk. This is the method for calculating an installation: list the loads, add them up, and deduce the line current I = S/U (single-phase) or S/(√3U) (three-phase).

📈1.5 Resonance in electric circuits

Series resonance (voltage resonance)

In a series R-L-C circuit, Z = R + j(XL − XC). When XL = XC, the imaginary part vanishes: the circuit is purely resistive, the current is maximal and in phase with the voltage, and UL = −UC.

LCω₀² = 1   ⇒   ω₀ = 1/√(LC)    f₀ = 1/(2π√(LC))     ρ = L ω₀ = 1/(Cω₀) = √(L/C)    (characteristic impedance)

The overvoltage coefficient (or quality factor) measures the voltage that appears across the reactive elements:

qu = UL/U = UC/U = ρ/R

With a q of 10, a 230 V source produces 2,300 V across the capacitor: this is the danger of series resonance, and the point of tuned circuits in radio frequency.

Series R-L-C circuit and its Fresnel diagram. At resonance XL = XC: UL and UC cancel each other, U = Ur and the current is maximal.

Parallel resonance (current resonance, antiresonance)

For a coil (R₁, L) in parallel with a capacitor (R₂, C), the admittance is written Y = g − jb with

g = R₁/(R₁² + (Lω)²) + R₂/(R₂² + (1/Cω)²)     b = Lω/(R₁² + (Lω)²) − (1/Cω)/(R₂² + (1/Cω)²)

Resonance (b = 0) gives:

ω′₀ = (1/√(LC))·√[(L/C − R₁²)/(L/C − R₂²)] = ω₀·√[(ρ² − R₁²)/(ρ² − R₂²)]

It is possible only if R₁ and R₂ are both smaller than ρ, or both larger. If R₁ = R₂ ≠ ρ, we get ω′₀ = ω₀ back; if R₁ = R₂ = ρ, the circuit resonates at all frequencies. At resonance, the line current is minimal (limited by R) while the currents in L and C can be considerable: the overcurrent factor is qi = IL/I = IC/I.

🔺1.6 Three-phase systems

A balanced three-phase system consists of three voltages of the same RMS value, shifted by 120°: Va = V∠0°, Vb = V∠−120°, Vc = V∠+120° (positive sequence). The phase voltages V (phase-to-neutral) are measured between phase and neutral, the line voltages U (phase-to-phase) between phases: U = √3·V (230/400 V in low voltage).

For the same impedance per branch, the delta absorbs 3 times the power of the star. When balanced, the neutral current is zero (sum of three equal phasors shifted by 120°): a single phase is studied (equivalent single-phase diagram, called “single-line”), as NRJXpert does in load flow and short-circuit calculations. When unbalanced (single-phase loads), the neutral carries the sum of the phase currents.

Where the three phases come from

A turn of area S₀ rotating at speed ω in a magnetic field B sees a flux Φ = B·S₀·cos(ωt + Ψ) and is the seat of an EMF e = −dΦ/dt = B·S₀·ω·sin(ωt + Ψ): sinusoidal. If three coils shifted by 120° in space are placed around the same rotor (electromagnet), three EMFs shifted by 120° in time are obtained:

eA = Em·sin(ωt)    eB = Em·sin(ωt − 2π/3)    eC = Em·sin(ωt + 2π/3)   ⇒   eA + eB + eC = 0    and    EA + EB + EC = 0

The phase sequence (or order) is the order in which the maxima follow one another: A-B-C (positive sequence) or A-C-B (negative sequence) — it determines the direction of rotation of induction motors. Three-phase power, discovered in 1890-1891, prevailed because it provides two voltages (phase and line), transmits the same power with less copper, and produces a rotating field: the induction motor, the most widespread machine in industry.

Relations between voltages and currents

The isosceles triangle formed by two phase voltages gives UBC = 2·VC·cos 30° = √3·V; likewise, in delta, the line current is the difference of two branch currents shifted by 120°:

Star: U = √3·V    I = J       Delta: U = V    I = √3·J
The two connections. In star, each branch receives the phase voltage V = U/√3 and carries the line current; in delta, it receives the line voltage U and the line current is √3·J.

Calculating a 4-wire system (star with neutral)

Generator, line and receiver, balanced or not: Millman's theorem is applied between the receiver neutral O′ and the generator neutral O:

UO′O = (EAYA + EBYB + ECYC) / (YA + YB + YC + YN)
with YA = 1/(ZOA + ZL + ZA′) … and YN = 1/ZN
IA = (EA − UO′O)·YA     (same for B and C)     IN = −UO′O·YN

If the system is balanced (YA = YB = YC and ΣE = 0), then UO′O = 0 and IN = 0: the neutral wire can be removed and the study reduces to a single phase. It is this equivalent single-phase diagram — the single-line diagram — that NRJXpert uses for load flow, short circuit and DER studies.

When unbalanced, on the contrary, UO′O ≠ 0: the receiver neutral shifts, the three phase voltages are no longer equal, and the neutral current is ΣIk. If the neutral is cut, some phases are overvoltaged (exercise 9): this is the classic incident on low-voltage networks with poorly distributed single-phase loads.

Calculating a 3-wire system

Star receiver — the line voltages are known (they always satisfy UAB + UBC + UCA = 0):

VA = (UABYB + UACYC)/(YA+YB+YC)    VB = (UBAYA + UBCYC)/(ΣY)    VC = (UCAYA + UCBYB)/(ΣY)

Delta receiver — the branch voltages are the line voltages:

JAB = UAB/ZAB …     IA = JAB − JCA    IB = JBC − JAB    IC = JCA − JBC

If the line impedance is not negligible, the delta is first transformed into its equivalent star (Kennelly, § 1.2), then UO′O and the line currents are calculated, and one returns to the branch currents through UA′B′ = IAZA′ − IBZB′.

Three-phase power

P = Σ VkJkcos φk    Q = Σ VkJksin φk    S = √(P² + Q²)    (general case)
Balanced: P = 3·V·J·cos φ = √3·U·I·cos φ    Q = √3·U·I·sin φ    S = √3·U·I

Warning: when unbalanced, S = √3·U·I no longer makes sense, and cos φ is no longer unique — calculate phase by phase (or, better, with the symmetrical components of § 1.7).

Two-wattmeter method (3-wire network): the current circuits are inserted in two phases, the voltage circuits connected to the third. P = PW1 + PW2, whether the load is balanced or not, with no access to the neutral; a wattmeter reads a negative value if cos φ < 0.5.

Why two wattmeters are enough. In a 3-wire system, iA + iB + iC = 0, so iC = −iA − iB and

p = vAiA + vBiB + vCiC = (vA − vC)·iA + (vB − vC)·iB = uAC·iA + uBC·iB

The average gives P = UACIAcos(UAC,IA) + UBCIBcos(UBC,IB), that is, the sum of the readings of the two wattmeters — whether the receiver is balanced or not, and without any access to the neutral. In the balanced case only:

PW1 = U·I·cos(φ − 30°)    PW2 = U·I·cos(φ + 30°)   ⇒   PW1 + PW2 = √3·U·I·cos φ = P    and    PW1 − PW2 = U·I·sin φ = Q/√3

Practical consequence: for |φ| > 60° (cos φ < 0.5), one of the two wattmeters reads a negative value; its current circuit must be reversed and its reading counted as negative. A negative wattmeter is therefore not a fault.

🔱1.7 Symmetrical components

An unbalanced network can no longer be calculated on a single phase. The method of symmetrical components (Fortescue, 1918) reduces it to three balanced networks, each calculable on one phase.

Theorem. Any system of three sinusoidal quantities of the same nature and the same frequency is the superposition of three balanced systems: a positive-sequence system (subscript d or 1), a negative-sequence system (i or 2) and a zero-sequence system (0), with the rotation operator a = ej2π/3 (a³ = 1, 1 + a + a² = 0):

positive: A₁ = Kd, B₁ = a²Kd, C₁ = aKd     negative: A₂ = Ki, B₂ = aKi, C₂ = a²Ki     zero: A₀ = B₀ = C₀ = K₀
Kd = ⅓(A + aB + a²C)    Ki = ⅓(A + a²B + aC)    K₀ = ⅓(A + B + C)     (Fortescue matrix)
Fortescue decomposition: any system of three phasors is the sum of a positive-sequence system, a negative-sequence system (phase order reversed) and a zero-sequence system (three coincident phasors, which flow only if a return path exists).

Physical interpretation: the positive-sequence component is the “normal” system, the one that makes motors turn in the right direction; the negative-sequence component creates a field rotating the other way (heating and braking torque in machines: the ratio Vi/Vd is limited to 2 % in operation); the zero-sequence component is in phase on all three phases and can flow only if a return path exists (neutral wire or earth): IN = 3·I₀.

Sequence impedances

A balanced receiver of impedance Z, with its neutral connected through ZN, subjected to three arbitrary voltages, is calculated sequence by sequence:

Id = Vd/Z    Ii = Vi/Z    I₀ = V₀/(Z + 3ZN)   ⇒   I₁ = Id + Ii + I₀, I₂ = a²Id + aIi + I₀, …

The factor 3ZN comes from the three zero-sequence currents returning together through the neutral. For machines and transformers, Z₀ differs strongly from Zd (and depends on the connection: a delta winding blocks the zero-sequence): these are the R0, X0 and zeroSequence data entered in NRJXpert.

Compensation theorem and equations

To handle a fault (or any localised asymmetry), the asymmetric element is replaced by three voltage sources equal to the voltage drops across it (compensation theorem: a resistor carrying ik is equivalent to an EMF Ek = Rkik oriented opposite to the current). By decomposing these sources and applying superposition, three balanced diagrams and three general equations are obtained:

① E1eq = I₁·Z1eq + V₁     ② 0 = I₂·Z2eq + V₂     ③ 0 = I₀·Z0eq + V₀

Only the positive-sequence networks contain sources (alternators produce only a positive-sequence system). These three equations are not enough: they are completed by the fault equations.

Motors: P = 3 × 11/0.9 = 36.67 kW; Q = 36.67 × tan(31.79°) = 22.72 kvar. Total: P = 42.67 kW, Q = 22.72 kvar, S = 48.34 kVA; cos φ = 0.883; I = 48,340/(√3 × 400) = 69.8 A.

Exercise 4 — With NRJXpert: voltage divider and Millman

Draw example 1.2 (two DC sources with series resistance, 6 Ω load) and check V = 9 V. Replace E₂ by 8 V: what is the sign of this source's current? Interpret.

Show solution

V = (6 + 8/3)/1 = 8.667 V; I₂ = (8 − 8.667)/3 = −0.222 A: the 8 V source receives current (it behaves as a receiver). The software displays this current as negative (or 180° out of phase) in the direction of the source.

Exercise 5 — Coil and capacitor in series

A coil (r, L) in series with a capacitor C carries I = 2 A at 50 Hz. The measurements are U = 100 V across the assembly, UB = 150 V across the coil and UC = 200 V across the capacitor. Determine r, L and C.

Show solution

The capacitor is purely reactive: XC = UC/I = 100 Ω → C = 1/(314.16 × 100) = 31.8 µF.

Two equations for the coil: (rI)² + (XLI)² = UB² = 150² and (rI)² + (XLI − XCI)² = U² = 100². Subtracting: 2·XC·XL·I² = 150² − 100² + (XCI)² → XL = 65.6 Ω → L = 209 mH; then r = √((150/2)² − 65.6²) = 36.3 Ω.

Check: Z = 36.3 + j(65.6 − 100) = 50∠−43.4° Ω, i.e. U = 50 × 2 = 100 V ✔. Note that UB + UC = 350 V ≫ U = 100 V: the voltages add vectorially, and the circuit is capacitive even though it contains a large inductance.

Exercise 6 — AC network: meshes, nodes and power balance

Three branches in parallel between two nodes. Branch 1: E₁ = 120∠30° V, r₁ = 3 Ω, XL1 = 8 Ω, XC1 = 4 Ω. Branch 2: E₂ = 220∠30° V, r₂ = 6 Ω, XL2 = 16 Ω, XC2 = 8 Ω. Branch 3 (no source): r₃ = 12 Ω, XL3 = 32 Ω, XC3 = 16 Ω. (a) Calculate the currents by the independent-mesh method. (b) Find them again by the node-potential method. (c) Calculate the voltages across each element and draw up the power balance. (d) Repeat with a current source J = 10∠−23.1° A injected at the upper node.

Exercise 6: three branches between two nodes. E₁ = 120∠30° V (r₁ = 3 Ω, XL1 = 8 Ω, XC1 = 4 Ω); E₂ = 220∠30° V (6, 16, 8 Ω); passive branch 3 (12, 32, 16 Ω). Both sources deliver towards the upper node: I₁ + I₂ = I₃. Question (d): the current source J is injected at the upper node.
Show solution

Impedances: Z₁ = 3 + j4 = 5∠53.13°; Z₂ = 6 + j8 = 10∠53.13°; Z₃ = 12 + j16 = 20∠53.13° Ω (same argument: all the currents will be shifted by the same angle).

(a) Meshes. Two independent meshes (1-3 and 2-3), with currents II and III both flowing through Z₃:

(Z₁ + Z₃)·II + Z₃·III = E₁     Z₃·II + (Z₂ + Z₃)·III = E₂

i.e., in magnitude, [25 20; 20 30]·[I] = [120; 220] at ∠(30° − 53.13°): II = −2.286∠−23.13° = 2.286∠156.87° A and III = 8.857∠−23.13° A; I₃ = II + III = 6.571∠−23.13° A.

(b) Nodes (Millman, a single independent node): U = (E₁Y₁ + E₂Y₂)/(Y₁+Y₂+Y₃) = (24 + 22)∠−23.13°/0.35∠−53.13° = 131.4∠30° V. Then I₁ = (E₁ − U)Y₁ = 2.286∠156.87° A, I₂ = (E₂ − U)Y₂ = 8.857∠−23.13° A, I₃ = U·Y₃ = 6.571∠−23.13° A: identical.

(c) Voltages and balance. Branch 1: Ur = 6.86 V, UL = 18.29 V, UC = 9.14 V; branch 2: 53.1 V, 141.7 V, 70.9 V; branch 3: 78.9 V, 210.3 V, 105.1 V. Power supplied: S = E₁I₁* + E₂I₂* = 1,004.6 W + j1,339.4 var; power received: ΣrkIk² = 1,004.6 W and ΣXkIk² = 1,339.4 var ✔. Source E₁ is acting as a receiver (its current is at 156.87° from its EMF): it absorbs 164.6 W supplied by E₂.

(d) With the current source: U = (E₁Y₁ + E₂Y₂ + J)/ΣY = 160∠30° V; I₁ = 8∠156.87° A, I₂ = 6∠−23.13° A, I₃ = 8∠−23.13° A (node law: −8 + 6 + 10 = 8 ✔). Balance: S = E₁I₁* + E₂I₂* + U·J* = 1,176 W + j1,568 var = ΣZkIk² ✔.

Fresnel diagram: place U at 30°, the three currents at −23.13° (and 156.87° for I₁); for each branch, Ur is in phase with the current, UL leads by 90°, UC lags by 90°, and their vector sum reaches E and U.

Exercise 7 — Single-phase workshop: compensation and voltage drop

A 50 Hz single-phase line (rL = 0.3 Ω, lL = 4 mH) supplies, at Vr = 230 V, a workshop made up of: 20 incandescent lamps of 250 W; a 4 kW motor (mechanical power) with efficiency 0.85 and cos φ = 0.8; an electromagnet Z = 5 + jLω with L = 100 mH. (1) Line current. (2) cos φ of the workshop. (3) Expression of the new power factor after connecting a capacitor C. (4) Value of C for cos φ = 1. (5-6) Voltage Vg at the sending end of the line, without then with compensation.

Show solution

Inventory (Boucherot). Lamps: P = 5,000 W, Q = 0. Motor: P = 4,000/0.85 = 4,706 W, S = 5,882 VA, Q = 3,529 var. Electromagnet: Z = 5 + j31.42 = 31.81∠80.96° Ω → I = 7.23 A, P = 261 W, Q = 1,642 var.

(1-2) Ptot = 9,967 W, Qtot = 5,172 var, S = 11,229 VA → I = 11,229/230 = 48.8 A and cos φ = 0.888 (inductive).

(3) The capacitor supplies QC = CωVr². The power triangle becomes (Ptot; Qtot − QC):

cos φ₁ = Ptot / √[ Ptot² + (Ptot·tan φ − 2πf·C·Vr²)² ]

(4) cos φ₁ = 1 ⇔ QC = Qtot → C = 5,172/(314.16 × 230²) = 311 µF.

(5) ZL = 0.3 + j1.257 Ω. Without compensation, I = 48.8∠−27.4° A (phase reference: Vr): Vg = Vr + ZLI = 275.4∠9.98° V, i.e. 19.7 % above 230 V.

(6) With compensation, I = 9,967/230 = 43.3 A in phase: Vg = 249.0∠12.63° V (8.3 %). The line losses fall from 715 W to 563 W (−21 %). Compensation reduces the current, the voltage drop and the losses — which is exactly what NRJXpert calculates for a distribution feeder (level 5, Q(V) control).

Exercise 8 — Connections of a three-phase load

(a) Three resistors connected in delta consume 60 kW. What power do they absorb in star? (b) Three 15 Ω resistors and three 8 Ω reactances are connected symmetrically to a 530 V network as follows: (1) R and X in series, in star; (2) R and X in parallel, in delta; (3) R in delta and X in star. Calculate the line current in each case.

Show solution

(a) In star, each branch receives U/√3 instead of U: the power is divided by 3 → 20 kW. (Kennelly's rule: RΔ = 3RY.)

(b1) Z = 15 + j8 = 17 Ω ; V = 530/√3 = 306 V → I = 18,0 A.

(b2) Z = 15·(j8)/(15 + j8) = 7.06∠61.9° Ω; branch current J = 530/7.06 = 75.1 A → I = √3 × 75.1 = 130.0 A.

(b3) Two loads in parallel: the delta-connected resistors draw IR = √3 × 530/15 = 61.2 A, in phase with the voltage; the star-connected reactances draw IX = 306/8 = 38.2 A, lagging by 90°. Hence I = √(61.2² + 38.2²) = 72.2 A and cos φ = 0.848.

Exercise 9 — Neutral break

A 4-wire 380/220 V network supplies three star-connected resistors: R₁ = 10 Ω, R₂ = 11 Ω, R₃ = 22 Ω. Calculate the voltages and currents (a) with the neutral conductor, (b) after the neutral breaks.

Show solution

(a) Each resistor receives 220 V: I₁ = 22 A, I₂ = 20 A, I₃ = 10 A. Neutral current: IN = I₁ + I₂ + I₃ = 11.1∠−51.1° A — not negligible, even though the loads are purely resistive.

(b) The neutral point shifts (Millman): VN′ = ΣVkGk/ΣGk = 47.1∠−51.1° V. The voltages across the resistors become 193.9 V; 207.8 V; 266.6 V and the currents 19.4 A; 18.9 A; 12.1 A.

The weakest load (R₃ = 22 Ω, the least power-consuming) is overvoltaged by 21 %: lamps or electronic devices connected there would be destroyed. This is the classic accident of a neutral break in low voltage.

Variant (factory). 400 V network: phase 1 — 100 A at cos φ = 1; phase 2 — 60 A at cos φ = 0.9 lagging; phase 3 — 200 A at cos φ = 0.8 lagging. Then IN = 181 A (almost as much as the most heavily loaded phase!), P = 72.5 kW, Q = 33.8 kvar, S = 80.0 kVA. After the neutral breaks (constant-impedance loads): 261.8 V; 340.2 V; 138.9 V — the most lightly loaded phase rises to 340 V.

Exercise 10 — Two-wattmeter method and faults

A 3-wire, 400 V / 50 Hz three-phase network supplies a balanced star load made up, per phase, of a resistor R = 20 Ω in series with a capacitor C = 20 µF. (1) Line currents, P, Q, S. (2) Readings of the two wattmeters. (3) Repeat the calculation if one phase of the load is short-circuited, then if one supply line breaks.

Show solution

(1) XC = 159.2 Ω; Z = 20 − j159.2 = 160.4∠−82.84° Ω; V = 231 V → I = 1.44 A; P = 3RI² = 124 W; Q = −3XCI² = −990 var (the load supplies reactive power); S = 997 VA.

(2) φ = −82.84°: PW1 = UIcos(φ − 30°) = −223.5 W and PW2 = UIcos(φ + 30°) = +347.9 W; sum = 124 W ✔. Since cos φ = 0.124 < 0.5, one of the wattmeters indeed reads a negative value. Reactive power check: √3(PW1 − PW2) = −990 var ✔ (valid here, the system being balanced).

(3a) Phase A short-circuited: the load neutral point is brought to the potential of phase A. The other two branches receive the line voltages: IB = IC = 400/160.4 = 2.49 A and IA = 4.32 A (√3 times more); P = 249 W (double).

(3b) Line break: the two remaining branches are in series across one line voltage: I = 400/(2 × 160.4) = 1.25 A; P = 2RI² = 62 W (half). A single wattmeter is then enough. Both cases can be drawn and checked directly in NRJXpert (delete a link, or connect the two terminals of a branch with a wire).

✅Level 1 quiz

1. Two resistors of 6 Ω and 3 Ω in parallel have an equivalent resistance of:

  1. 9 Ω
  2. 4,5 Ω
  3. 2 Ω
  4. 0,5 Ω
6 × 3/(6 + 3) = 2 Ω: the equivalent is always smaller than the smallest resistance.

2. A 10 mH inductor has, at 50 Hz, a reactance of:

  1. 0,5 Ω
  2. 3,14 Ω
  3. 31,4 Ω
  4. 500 Ω
X = 2πfL = 314,16 × 0,01 = 3,14 Ω.

3. In established DC steady state, a capacitor behaves as:

  1. an open circuit
  2. a short circuit
  3. a resistance 1/C
  4. an ideal voltage source
1/(jCω) becomes infinite as ω → 0: no steady current.

4. An inductive load absorbs:

  1. P < 0 et Q > 0
  2. P > 0 et Q < 0
  3. P = 0 et Q > 0
  4. P > 0 et Q > 0
In receiver convention, an R-L load absorbs active and reactive power; a capacitor “supplies” reactive power (Q < 0).

5. Compensating the reactive energy of an installation mainly makes it possible to:

  1. reduce the active power consumed
  2. reduce the current and therefore the line losses
  3. increase the frequency
  4. reduce the voltage
At constant P, I = P/(U·cos φ) decreases when cos φ increases.

6. On a 230/400 V network, the voltage between two phases is:

  1. 230 V
  2. 326 V
  3. 400 V
  4. 690 V
U = √3·V = 1,732 × 230,9 = 400 V.

7. With equal branch impedances, a delta load absorbs, compared with star:

  1. 3 times more power
  2. √3 times more
  3. the same
  4. 3 times less
Each branch receives U instead of U/√3: the power (in U²) is multiplied by 3.

8. The neutral current of a balanced three-phase star load is:

  1. equal to the line current
  2. zero
  3. √3 times the line current
  4. 3 times the line current
Ia + Ib + Ic = 0 for three equal phasors shifted by 120°.

9. The NRJXpert server calculates a linear network by:

  1. the mesh method
  2. Thévenin's theorem
  3. the Newton-Raphson method
  4. the node-potential method (Y·V = J)
Newton-Raphson is used for load flow (nonlinear power equations), not for the linear network.

10. 209.9 V across the load and 66.8 V across the line, for a 230 V source: why is the sum not 230 V?

  1. software rounding error
  2. losses in the line
  3. the voltages are out of phase and add up as complex numbers
  4. the frequency is not 50 Hz
The mesh law applies to phasors: |U1 + U2| ≤ |U1| + |U2|.

11. In the mesh-current method, the mutual resistance R12 carries a “−” sign when:

  1. the common branch contains a source
  2. the two mesh currents flow through the common branch in opposite directions
  3. mesh 1 is larger than mesh 2
  4. never
The sign reflects the relative orientation of the two mesh currents in the branch they share.

12. In the node-potential method, the “node current” Ja is:

  1. the sum of the unknown branch currents
  2. the short-circuit current of the node
  3. the sum of the E·G and J terms of the branches meeting at the node, oriented towards it
  4. always zero
Voltage sources are converted into equivalent current sources E·G (Norton model).

13. Three 30 Ω resistors in delta are equivalent, in star, to three resistors of:

  1. 10 Ω
  2. 30 Ω
  3. 90 Ω
  4. 17,3 Ω
Kennelly, balanced case: RΔ = 3RY.

14. The superposition theorem does not allow adding:

  1. branch currents
  2. voltages
  3. node potentials
  4. powers
P = RI² is quadratic: the currents must be superposed first, then the power calculated.

15. At resonance of a series R-L-C circuit with overvoltage coefficient q = 10 supplied at 230 V, the capacitor voltage is:

  1. 23 V
  2. 2 300 V
  3. 230 V
  4. 0 V
UC = q·U = ρU/R: this is the danger of series resonance.

16. The two-wattmeter method measures the active power of a 3-wire network:

  1. only if the load is balanced
  2. only if the load is star-connected
  3. in all cases, balanced or not
  4. only if cos φ > 0.5
The proof uses only iA + iB + iC = 0. On the other hand, √3(PW1 − PW2) = Q is true only when balanced.

17. The zero-sequence component of a system of currents can flow only if:

  1. a return path exists (neutral or earth)
  2. the system is balanced
  3. the load is delta-connected
  4. the frequency is 50 Hz
The three zero-sequence currents are in phase: their sum, IN = 3I₀, must be able to close.

18. After the neutral conductor breaks in an LV network with unbalanced single-phase loads:

  1. nothing changes
  2. all the voltages drop
  3. all the currents vanish
  4. the neutral point shifts: the most lightly loaded phase is overvoltaged

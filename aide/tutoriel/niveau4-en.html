📘Introduction

Objectives

  • Understand the six notions of project appraisal (inflation, discount rate, discounted cost, NPV, IRR, payback): the question each one answers and its decision criterion.
  • Discount cash flows, account for inflation, calculate an annuity.
  • Calculate the discounted cost of a project (investment, operation, replacements, residual value) and the cost of the kWh (LCOE).
  • Assess profitability: NPV, IRR, simple and discounted payback; determine the minimum tariff and subsidy.
  • Carry out a sensitivity analysis and compare solutions (PV, diesel, grid).

Prerequisites: level 3. Duration: 10 to 12 h.

🧭4.0 The six essential notions of project appraisal

Before calculating, you need to understand what each indicator measures and which question it answers. Six notions are enough to appraise an energy project: the inflation rate, the discount rate, the discounted cost, the NPV, the IRR and the payback period. They build on one another: the first two are assumptions you choose, the other four are results you calculate and interpret.

Running example. Mrs Diop runs a shop in Thiès. She is considering investing 1,000 kF in a small photovoltaic installation that would save her 250 kF per year on electricity and diesel for 10 years. Her bank lends at 8 %. Is the project worthwhile? Each notion below provides part of the answer.

1. The inflation rate: how much value does money lose?

Definition. The percentage general and sustained increase in the prices of goods and services over a period, usually one year. It measures the loss of purchasing power of money.

Intuition. With 3 % inflation, a basket of groceries that cost 100 last year costs 103 today: with the same 100 you buy less. Inflation makes nobody richer; it only changes the unit of measurement from one year to the next.

Price in year n: Cn = C₀ · (1 + i)n

Example 4.0.1 — Diesel in ten years

A litre of diesel costs 750 F. If prices rise by 4 % per year, how much will it cost in 10 years?

Show the solution

750 × 1.04¹⁰ = 1,110 F/l (+48 %). A genset burning 4,000 l/yr will see its bill rise from 3,000 kF to over 4,400 kF per year: inflation weighs heavily on solutions with high running costs, and little on a PV plant, most of which is paid on day one.

In NRJXpert. The "Inflation" field of the economic viability tabs (PV, wind) makes operating, fuel and replacement costs grow each year.

2. The discount rate: what is a future franc worth today?

Definition. The rate used to convert a future amount into its equivalent value today.

Intuition: 1 franc today is worth more than 1 franc tomorrow, for three reasons:

  • inflation: tomorrow that franc will buy less;
  • risk: the future may not go as planned (breakdowns, lower tariffs, unpaid bills);
  • opportunity cost: available today, the franc could be invested and earn interest.

In practice it is the minimum rate of return an investor requires before tying money up in a project (cost of borrowing, or a risk-free return plus a risk premium).

Present value: PV = Fn / (1 + d)n    discount factor: DFn = 1/(1 + d)n
Year n151020
Present value of 1,000 F received in year n, at 8 %926 F681 F463 F215 F

At 8 %, a franc received in 20 years "weighs" only 21 % of a franc today. The higher the rate, the less distant gains count — which penalises long-lived projects such as solar.

Example 4.0.2

You are promised 1,000,000 F in 5 years. What amount would you accept today if your discount rate is 8 %?

Show the solution

1,000,000/1.08⁵ = 680,583 F. Above that amount, you are better off taking the money now.

Nominal or real rate? If cash flows are in current francs (including inflation), discount at the nominal rate; if they are in constant francs (excluding inflation), discount at the real rate: (1 + dnominal) = (1 + dreal)(1 + i). Mixing the two is the most common mistake.

3. The discounted cost: how much will this project really cost in total?

Definition. The sum of all expenses of a project over its life — initial purchase, installation, maintenance, energy, repairs and replacements — brought back to today's value with the discount rate.

Cdisc = I₀ + Σn=1..N Cn / (1 + d)n

What is it for? To compare objectively two items that provide the same service: a machine that is cheap to buy but consumes a lot for 10 years may cost more than an expensive but efficient one.

Example 4.0.3 — Two refrigerators for a health post

Refrigerator A: 150 kF, 1.6 kWh/day. Refrigerator B: 350 kF, 0.6 kWh/day. Electricity at 120 F/kWh, 10 years, 8 % discount rate, constant prices. Which is cheaper?

Show the solution

Annual energy cost: A = 1.6 × 365 × 0.120 = 70.08 kF; B = 0.6 × 365 × 0.120 = 26.28 kF. Sum of discount factors over 10 years at 8 %: Σ 1/1.08n = 6.710.

Cdisc(A) = 150 + 70.08 × 6.710 = 620 kF; Cdisc(B) = 350 + 26.28 × 6.710 = 526 kF. Refrigerator B, more than twice as expensive to buy, saves 94 kF in today's money — and it also relieves the PV system that powers it.

In NRJXpert. The discounted cost of a system (investment, operation, battery and inverter replacements, residual value) is computed in the "Economic viability" tab; divided by the discounted energy, it gives the LCOE (section 4.3).

4. NPV: how much money does this project make me?

Definition. The net present value is the net wealth created by a project, expressed in today's money: add up all discounted future gains and subtract the initial investment.

NPV = sum of discounted future gains − initial investment = −I₀ + Σn=1..N Fn / (1 + d)n
ResultInterpretationDecision
NPV > 0the project earns more than the minimum required by the discount rateinvest
NPV = 0it just repays the investment and the required return, with no surplusindifferent
NPV < 0the project destroys valueabandon it (or seek a subsidy, section 4.5)

Example 4.0.4 — Mrs Diop's project

Investment 1,000 kF, savings 250 kF/yr for 10 years, rate 8 %. NPV?

Show the solution

NPV = −1,000 + 250 × 6.710 = +678 kF. The project is profitable: on top of repaying her 8 % loan, Mrs Diop gains 678 kF in today's money.

5. IRR: what is the annual return of this project?

Definition. The internal rate of return is the project's actual annual rate of return. Mathematically, it is the exact discount rate that makes the NPV zero.

IRR: Σn=0..N Fn / (1 + IRR)n = 0   (found by trial and error or bisection)

How to interpret it? Compare it directly with the discount rate or the interest rate of a loan:

  • if the bank lends at 4 % and the IRR is 9 %, the project is very attractive: it yields a 5-point margin;
  • if the IRR is below the discount rate, the project is not profitable enough for the risk taken.

Example 4.0.5

What is the IRR of Mrs Diop's project?

Show the solution

Find r such that 250 × Σn=1..10 1/(1 + r)n = 1,000, i.e. a sum of discount factors equal to 4. It is 4.19 at 20 % and 3.92 at 22 %: by bisection, IRR ≈ 21.4 %, well above the 8 % loan rate.

Limits. When cash flows change sign several times (costly battery replacement mid-life), there may be several IRRs or none: NPV then remains the reference criterion. Between two mutually exclusive projects of different sizes, the higher IRR is not necessarily the higher NPV.

6. Payback period: how many years to recover my outlay?

Definition. The time (in years or months) needed for the project's cumulative gains to repay the initial outlay exactly. Note: in French the acronym TRI is sometimes also used for "temps de retour sur investissement" (payback); in NRJXpert, TRI always means internal rate of return.

Simple payback = I₀ / annual gain    discounted payback: first year in which Σ Fn/(1 + d)n ≥ I₀

Example 4.0.6

Simple and discounted payback of Mrs Diop's project?

Show the solution

Simple: 1,000/250 = 4 years. Discounted: cumulative discounted savings are 828 kF after 4 years, 998 kF after 5 years and 1,156 kF after 6 years: ≈ 5.0 years. Discounting always lengthens the payback period.

Limits. It is a very simple risk and liquidity indicator, but incomplete: it ignores what happens after payback (a project that is slow to pay back may earn a great deal over 20 years) and, in its simple form, discounting.

In summary: how do the six notions fit together?

IndicatorQuestion it answersDecision criterion
InflationHow much value does money lose?calculation basis (growth of future costs)
Discount rateWhat is a future franc worth today?used to compute NPV and discounted cost
Discounted costHow much will this project really cost in total?choose the lowest cost (for the same service)

Four-step method

  1. Set the assumptions: study period, inflation, discount rate (nominal or real, consistently with the cash flows).
  2. If the options provide the same service (supplying a village: PV, diesel or grid), compute their discounted cost or LCOE and keep the lowest.
  3. If the project generates revenue or savings, compute the NPV: if positive, it is profitable; then the IRR to compare with the cost of credit.
  4. Assess the risk with the payback period and a sensitivity analysis (section 4.6): what happens to the NPV if the tariff falls by 20 % or the rate rises to 12 %?
Pitfalls to avoid. (1) Discounting constant-franc flows at a nominal rate (or the reverse). (2) Forgetting replacements (batteries every 5–10 years, inverters every 10 years). (3) Comparing by NPV two projects with different lifetimes: compare their annuities or LCOE instead. (4) Believing that a short payback proves profitability. (5) Confusing the IRR (a rate, in %) with the payback period (a duration, in years).

🔹4.1 Time value of money

A franc available today is worth more than a franc in ten years: it can be invested, and the future is uncertain. The discount rate a expresses this preference (cost of capital of the investor, 6 to 12 % in West Africa).

Present value of an amount Fn paid in year n: Fn/(1 + a)n
Future cost with inflation i: Cn = C₀·(1 + i)n    real rate: (1 + a)/(1 + i) − 1
Constant annuity repaying I in N years: A = I·CRF,   CRF = a(1 + a)N/((1 + a)N − 1)

Example 4.1

(a) Present value of 1 MF spent in 10 years at 8 %? (b) Annuity of a 3 000 kF investment over 20 years at 8 %? (c) Real rate for a = 8 % and i = 3 %?

Show the solution

(a) 1 000/1.08¹⁰ = 463 kF. (b) CRF = 0.10185; A = 305.6 kF/yr. (c) 1.08/1.03 − 1 = 4.85 %.

💰4.2 Life-cycle cost and discounted cost

The costs of a system over its operating period N include:

  • the investment I₀: equipment, installation, wiring, civil works;
  • annual operation and maintenance (O&M), increased by inflation;
  • the replacements of equipment whose lifetime is shorter than N (batteries 5 years, electronics 10 years);
  • the fuel for a genset;
  • deducted, the residual value of recent equipment at the end of the project (straight-line depreciation).
Discounted cost (NPV of costs): Cdisc = I₀ + Σn=1..N (O&Mn + Rn + fueln)/(1 + a)n − Vres/(1 + a)N

NRJXpert also shows the « life-cycle cost » of the original program (depreciation annuities over the depreciation period + undiscounted operating costs): useful to compare variants of the same project, but only the discounted cost is comparable with published studies.

💰4.3 Levelised cost of energy (LCOE)

LCOE = Cdisc / Σn=1..N En/(1 + a)n

Energy is discounted like money: a kWh produced in 15 years is « worth » less than a kWh produced next year. The LCOE is the constant selling price that makes the project just profitable (NPV = 0). It allows technologies with different lifetimes and cost profiles to be compared. For a stand-alone system, E is the useful energy (consumed by the appliances), not the energy produced.

Example 4.2 — 10 kWp grid-connected plant

Investment 5 000 kF (500 kF/kWp); O&M 150 kF/yr (year-0 prices, inflation 3 %); inverters replaced at 10 years (600 kF); production 15 810 kWh in the first year (example 3.5), decreasing by 0.5 %/yr; 20 years; discount rate 8 %.

Show the solution

Present value of O&M: Σ 150 × 1.03n/1.08n = 1 892.6 kF; of the replacement: 600 × 1.03¹⁰/1.08¹⁰ = 373.5 kF. Cdisc = 5 000 + 1 892.6 + 373.5 = 7 266 kF.

Discounted energy: Σ 15 810 × 0.995n−1/1.08n = 149 901 kWh. LCOE = 7 266/149 901 = 0.0485 kF/kWh = 48.5 F/kWh.

📙4.4 NPV, IRR and payback

Cash flow of year n: Fn = revenuen − costsn (F₀ = −I₀)    NPV = Σn=0..N Fn/(1 + a)n
IRR: rate that cancels the NPV    payback: year when the cumulated cash flows (discounted or not) become positive
  • NPV > 0: the project earns more than an investment at rate a; between two mutually exclusive projects, the larger NPV is preferred.
  • IRR > a: equivalent to NPV > 0 for a single project; convenient to compare with the cost of credit.
  • Payback: risk (liquidity) indicator; it ignores what happens afterwards.

Example 4.3 — Profitability of the 10 kWp plant

The energy is sold (or saved) at 60 F/kWh, constant tariff.

Show the solution

Cash flow of year 1: 15 810 × 0.060 − 150 × 1.03 = 794 kF; year 10: negative (−101 kF) because of the inverter replacement. NPV = +1 728 kF; IRR = 12.8 %; payback 6.5 years (discounted: 11.0 years). The project is profitable at the 8 % rate.

🧩4.5 Minimum tariff and subsidy

The NPV is a linear function of the tariff and the subsidy; they are therefore obtained exactly (NRJXpert does not scan):

Minimum tariff (NPV = 0) = LCOE (if revenue follows the energy)    Minimum subsidy = max(0, −NPVwithout subsidy/I₀)

Example 4.4

For the 10 kWp plant: minimum tariff? If the tariff is only 40 F/kWh, what share of the investment must be subsidised?

Show the solution

Minimum tariff = LCOE = 48.5 F/kWh. At 40 F/kWh, NPV = −1 270 kF: minimum subsidy = 1 270/5 000 = 25.4 %. Check: NPV(50 F/kWh) = +229 kF, NPV(60) = +1 728 kF, NPV(70) = +3 227 kF: the NPV increases by 150 kF per F/kWh (= discounted energy × 1 F).

🎯Exercises

Exercise 1 — Replacing a genset

A genset consumes 4 000 l/yr of diesel (750 F/l) and costs 400 kF/yr in maintenance. A PV-battery system of 18 MF would replace it entirely, with 300 kF/yr of maintenance and 6 MF of batteries to replace in years 7 and 14. Over 20 years at 8 % (without inflation), what is the NPV of the operation (savings − costs)?

Show the solution

Annual saving: 3 000 + 400 − 300 = 3 100 kF; Σ 1/1.08n (20 years) = 9.818 → 30 437 kF. Replacements: 6 000 × (1/1.08⁷ + 1/1.08¹⁴) = 6 000 × (0.5835 + 0.3405) = 5 544 kF. NPV = −18 000 + 30 437 − 5 544 = +6 893 kF. Residual value of the year-14 batteries (1/7 of life remaining) neglected: it would improve the result further.

Exercise 2 — Effect of the rate

With the calculator above (investment 10 000 kF, O&M 200 kF/yr, 15 000 kWh/yr, 20 years), from which discount rate does the LCOE exceed 100 F/kWh?

Show the solution

LCOE = (10 000·CRF + 200)/15 000 ≤ 0.1 kF/kWh ⇔ CRF ≤ 0.13. CRF(20 years) = 0.13 for a ≈ 11.5 %.

Exercise 3 — Interpreting a payback period

Two projects have the same NPV; the first pays back in 4 years, the second in 9 years. Which one should be chosen and why?

Show the solution

For equal NPV, the one that pays back faster exposes the investor for a shorter time (risk of failure, of tariff change, of exchange rate). The payback period separates projects that the NPV does not distinguish.

Exercise 4 — Inflation and real rate

Maintenance of an installation costs 200 kF/yr today; inflation is 4 % and the nominal discount rate 10 %. (a) What is the real rate? (b) What is the present value of maintenance over the next 10 years? Compute it in two ways.

Show the solution

(a) 1.10/1.04 − 1 = 5.77 %. (b) In current francs at the nominal rate: Σ 200 × 1.04n/1.10n; in constant francs at the real rate: 200 × Σ 1/1.0577n. Both give 1,488 kF: flows and rate are consistent. Classic mistake: 200 × Σ 1/1.10n = 1,229 kF (constant prices discounted at the nominal rate), 17 % too low.

Exercise 5 — IRR and cost of credit

A project costs 2,000 kF and earns 400 kF/yr for 8 years. (a) NPV at 8 % and at 10 %? (b) IRR? (c) Should it be financed with a loan at 10 %? at 13 %?

Show the solution

(a) NPV(8 %) = −2,000 + 400 × 5.747 = +299 kF; NPV(10 %) = −2,000 + 400 × 5.335 = +134 kF. (b) IRR ≈ 11.8 %. (c) At 10 %, yes (IRR > cost of credit, 1.8-point margin, thin: check sensitivity); at 13 %, no: the project would not cover the interest.

Exercise 6 — The payback trap

Project A: 1,000 kF invested, 500 kF/yr for 3 years. Project B: 1,000 kF invested, 250 kF/yr for 10 years. Rate 8 %. Compare their payback periods and NPVs. Which one should be chosen?

Show the solution

Payback: A = 2 years, B = 4 years. NPV: A = −1,000 + 500 × 2.577 = +289 kF; B = +678 kF. B creates more than twice as much value although it pays back twice as slowly: payback ignores what happens afterwards. Choose B, unless cash is very tight or the risk beyond 4 years is high.

📋Level 4 quiz

1. At 8 %, 1 000 kF paid in 10 years is worth today:

  1. 800 kF
  2. 463 kF
  3. 1 000 kF
  4. 2 159 kF
1 000/1.08¹⁰ = 463 kF; 2 159 kF is the future value of 1 000 kF invested for 10 years.

2. The LCOE is:

  1. the investment divided by the power
  2. the price of grid electricity
  3. the discounted cost divided by the discounted energy
  4. the energy cost of the first year
It is the constant tariff that cancels the NPV.

3. For a stand-alone system, the LCOE is calculated on:

  1. the useful energy consumed by the appliances
  2. the energy produced by the array
  3. the battery capacity
  4. the peak power
The surplus that is not consumed has no value.

4. A project has an IRR of 12 % and the discount rate is 8 %. Then:

  1. NPV < 0
  2. NPV = 0
  3. nothing can be said
  4. NPV > 0
NPV(a) > 0 for a < IRR (conventional project: an investment followed by positive cash flows).

5. The residual value at the end of the project:

  1. is added to the costs
  2. is deducted from the costs
  3. only affects the IRR
  4. is always zero
Equipment bought shortly before the end of the project still has value.

6. The cost of the kWh of a PV plant is mainly sensitive to:

  1. the price of diesel
  2. inflation
  3. the discount rate
  4. the power factor
Capital-intensive energy: the cost of capital dominates.

7. If the NPV without subsidy is −1 270 kF for an investment of 5 000 kF, the minimum subsidy is:

  1. 25.4 %
  2. 12.7 %
  3. 74.6 %
  4. 100 %
The subsidy reduces the year-0 investment, not discounted: 1 270/5 000.

8. The discounted payback period is:

  1. always shorter than the simple payback
  2. equal to the IRR
  3. independent of the rate
  4. longer than the simple payback
Future cash flows are reduced by discounting: more years are needed to offset the investment.

9. With 3 % inflation, a basket that cost 100 last year costs today:

  1. 97
  2. 100
  3. 103
  4. 130
100 × 1.03 = 103: with the same 100 you buy less (loss of purchasing power).

10. A zero NPV means the project:

  1. earns nothing at all
  2. just repays the investment and the required return, with no surplus
  3. destroys value
  4. has a zero IRR
At NPV = 0 the project earns exactly the discount rate: its IRR equals that rate.

11. The bank lends at 4 %; the project's IRR is 9 %. Then:

  1. the project is attractive: a 5-point margin
  2. an IRR of at least 13 % is needed
  3. the NPV at 4 % is negative
  4. no conclusion is possible without the payback
IRR > cost of credit: the project repays the interest and leaves a margin; the NPV at 4 % is positive.

12. Which indicator ignores what happens after the initial outlay is recovered?

  1. NPV
  2. IRR
  3. discounted cost
  4. payback period
Payback stops at recovery: a project slow to pay back may earn a lot afterwards.

13. To choose between two refrigerators providing the same service, compare:

  1. their purchase prices
  2. their IRRs
  3. their discounted life-cycle costs
  4. their first-year consumption
Discounted cost combines purchase, energy, maintenance and replacements in today's money: keep the lowest.

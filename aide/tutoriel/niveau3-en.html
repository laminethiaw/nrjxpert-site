☀️3.1 Solar resource

Solar geometry

The declination δ (angle between the equator and the direction of the sun) varies from −23.45° (21 December) to +23.45° (21 June): δ = 23.45·sin(360·(284 + n)/365), n = day number. The sunset hour angle is ωs = arccos(−tan φ·tan δ) (φ = latitude) and the day length 2ωs/15 hours.

H₀ = (24/π)·Gsc·(1 + 0.033 cos(360n/365))·(cos φ cos δ sin ωs + ωs sin φ sin δ)    Gsc = 1 367 W/m²
Clearness index Kt = H/H₀    diffuse fraction Hd/H = f(Kt) (Erbs correlation)

H₀ is the irradiation that would be received without the atmosphere; Kt measures the transparency of the sky (0.5 to 0.7 in the Sahel; lower in the rainy season and in dusty weather).

Tilted plane (Liu-Jordan, Klein)

Hβ = H·[ (1 − Hd/H)·Rb + (Hd/H)·(1 + cos β)/2 + ρground·(1 − cos β)/2 ]

The beam component is multiplied by Rb (geometric ratio tilted/horizontal), the diffuse by the fraction of sky seen, and the ground reflects a share ρground (albedo, 0.2; 0.3 on light sand).

☀️3.2 The photovoltaic module

A cell is a large illuminated diode: I = Iph − I₀(e(V + RsI)/(n·VT) − 1) − (V + RsI)/Rsh. Datasheets give, at standard test conditions (STC: 1 000 W/m², 25 °C, AM1.5): Voc, Isc, Vmp, Imp, Pp = Vmp·Imp (watt-peak) and the temperature coefficients.

Isc ∝ G    P ≈ Pp·(G/1000)·(1 + γ(Tc − 25))    γ ≈ −0.40 %/°C (crystalline silicon)
Tc = Ta + (NOCT − 20)/800 · G    (NOCT ≈ 45 °C)

Example 3.2 — 400 Wp module at noon in Dakar

G = 800 W/m², Ta = 35 °C, NOCT = 45 °C, γ = −0.40 %/°C.

Show the solution

Tc = 35 + 25/800 × 800 = 60 °C; P = 400 × 0.8 × (1 − 0.004 × 35) = 275 W: heat costs 14 %.

Connection. In series voltages add up (string); in parallel currents add up. A shaded module limits its whole string (bypass diodes). PWM charge controller: the modules work at the battery voltage (12, 24, 48 V); 36-cell modules (Vmp ≈ 17 V) are needed per 12 V. MPPT charge controller: a DC-DC converter makes the array work at its maximum power whatever the battery voltage; essential with current modules of 60 to 144 cells (Vmp 30 to 40 V).

☀️3.3 Sizing a stand-alone PV system

A stand-alone (off-grid) photovoltaic system consists of a PV array, one or more charge controllers, a battery, an inverter for the AC loads, and the cables between them. Sizing it means fixing the number of each item to meet two goals:

  • during the least sunny month, the array must produce the energy consumed every day, losses included;
  • the battery must supply the loads at night and during days without sun (a duration called the autonomy).

The calculation follows the energy from the user back to the sun: load demand → energy drawn from the battery → PV array → battery → charge controller → inverter → cables. Every quantity is defined in the table below.

Step 1 — Load assessment

For each load, note the number of appliances, their power and their operating hours. The mean daily energy is:

Ed = Σi ni·Pi·Ku·Ks/ηi · hi · ji/7   (Wh/d)

The factor ji/7 converts an appliance used only on some days (school closed at the weekend) to a mean day. The hourly profile (sum of the powers hour by hour) also gives Smax, the apparent power of the most loaded hour, which sets the size of the inverter.

Step 2 — Demand referred to the DC bus (ampere-hours)

All the energy passes through the DC bus: it leaves the battery or the controller, goes through the cables and, for AC loads, through the inverter. Each stage has an efficiency below 1, so the bus must supply more than Ed. Since the bus voltage is fixed, this demand is expressed in ampere-hours (the unit of batteries and controllers):

Qd = Σi Ed,i / (U·ηconv,i·ηcables·ηbat)   (Ah/d)      Q′d = Qd·(1 + m)

ηconv,i equals 1 for a DC load supplied directly at voltage U, and 0.9 otherwise. The margin m covers the uncertainty on the hours of use, demand growth and ageing; it is not a loss.

Step 3 — Design irradiation

Irradiation is converted to the tilted plane of the modules (§ 3.1). For a stand-alone system, size on the weakest month: if the array is enough that month, it is enough all year. Sizing on the annual mean gives a smaller array but months in deficit, to be covered by a generator. Hdes reads as peak-sun hours: a 1 Wp module produces about Hdes Wh per day, before losses.

Step 4 — Choosing the controller type: PWM or MPPT?

The controller protects the battery (it stops charging when the battery is full); its technology sets how the array operates, hence the array formula.

Step 5a — PV array with a PWM controller

An array delivering I amperes for H hours supplies I·H ampere-hours. The current to be supplied is therefore:

Iarray = Q′d / Hdes   (A)
Np = ⌈ Iarray / (kp·Imp) ⌉    strings in parallel (each string supplies Imp, reduced by the losses)
Ns = ⌈ 1.2·U / (0.9·Vmp) ⌉    modules per string

1.2·U is the end-of-charge voltage of the battery (14.4 V for 12 V): the string must exceed it even when heat lowers the module voltage, which the factor 0.9 accounts for. Monthly production: Eprod,m = U·Np·Imp·kp·Hm·dm.

Step 5b — PV array with an MPPT controller

The array is sized in power. The energy to be supplied to the bus every day is Q′d·U (Wh); one peak watt supplies Hdes·kp·fT·ηMPPT:

Pp,req = Q′d·U / (Hdes·kp·fT·ηMPPT)   (Wp)     fT = 1 + γ·(Ta + NOCT − 20 − 25)
Ns = ⌈ 1.3·U / (Vmp·(1 + 45·γ)) ⌉     Np = ⌈ Pp,req / (Ns·Pmod) ⌉

An MPPT controller can only step the voltage down: the string must stay above 1.3·U even with cells at 70 °C (45 °C above the 25 °C of STC, hence the factor 1 + 45·γ = 0.82). Check: the open-circuit voltage of the string in cold weather, 1.1·Ns·Voc, must stay below the maximum input voltage of the controller Vin,max. Monthly production: Eprod,m = Pp·Hm·kp·fT,m·ηMPPT·dm.

Step 6 — Battery

C = Q′bat · Aut / 24   (Ah)     Nbs = U / Ubat     Nbp = ⌈ C / (Cbat·DoD) ⌉     batteries = Nbs·Nbp

Q′bat equals Q′d, or counts only the priority loads if “Loads supplied by the battery: priority loads only” is chosen (the others are shed during the autonomy). Only DoD of the rated capacity is drawn to preserve the battery life: hence the division by DoD. The depth of discharge recommended by the database (AGM lead-acid 50 %, gel 60 %, OPzV 70 %, LFP lithium 90 %) replaces the project value. U must be a multiple of Ubat (12.8 V LFP counts as 12 V).

Step 7 — Charge controllers

A string is never shared between two controllers. For each controller, NRJXpert checks:

input current of a string = 1.25·Isc ≤ Iin,max     output current of a string = 1.25·Ns·Pmod/U (MPPT) or 1.25·Isc (PWM)
k = ⌊ min( Iin,max/(1.25·Isc) ; Ireg/output current of a string ) ⌋     Nreg = ⌈ Np / k ⌉

The factor 1.25 (guide UTE C15-712-1) covers irradiance above 1 000 W/m² (cloud edges, reflective ground). With PWM, the string voltage Vmp must not exceed 2·U, otherwise more than half of the power is lost.

Step 8 — Inverter

Ninv = ⌈ 1.25·Smax / Sinv ⌉    (stand-alone inverter whose DC voltage equals U)

The 25 % margin absorbs motor starting peaks (refrigerator, pump) and keeps the inverter from running at full load.

Step 9 — Month-by-month check and economic choice

For each month, NRJXpert compares the production Eprod,m with the demand seen from the bus Edem,m = Qd·U·dm:

surplus = Σ max(0, Eprod,m − Edem,m)    deficit = Σ max(0, Edem,m − Eprod,m)    τ = Σ min(Eprod,m, Edem,m) / Σ Edem,m

With the option “Choose the equipment with the lowest life-cycle cost”, every module, battery, controller and inverter of the database is tried; the module and the controller are chosen together (only connectable combinations count) and the combination kept is the cheapest over the whole operating life, replacements included (level 4). The wiring is then sized (§ 3.4).

All equipment counts are rounded up (rounding to the nearest integer undersizes: this was an error of the original program, corrected in NRJXpert). The examples of this tutorial are computed with a design margin of 0 % so that they can be checked by hand: set 0 to find their values.

  4. Voltage drop at rated current: ΔU = b·(ρL/S·cos φ + λL·sin φ)·I, b = 2 in DC and single-phase; maximum 3 % on the PV array side, 1 % on the battery side, 3 % on the AC side.
  5. Cross-section chosen = the larger of the two, standardised (1.5; 2.5; 4; 6; 10; 16; 25; 35; 50; 70; 95; 120; 150; 185; 240; 300 mm²).
  6. Cost = conductors (fixed part + part × cross-section, per metre of conductor) + installation (per metre of link) + protection; maintenance 1 %/yr; lifetime 25 years.

🌬️3.6 Wind resource and the Weibull distribution

The power of the wind crossing an area A is P = ½ρAv³: doubling the speed multiplies the power by 8. The speed fluctuates; its distribution generally follows a Weibull distribution:

f(v) = (k/c)(v/c)k−1 e−(v/c)k    F(v) = 1 − e−(v/c)k    v̄ = c·Γ(1 + 1/k)    power density = ½ρc³Γ(1 + 3/k)

c (m/s) is the scale parameter, k the shape parameter (k = 2: Rayleigh distribution; large k: steady wind, e.g. trade winds). NRJXpert fits c and k by maximum likelihood on the measurements, taking calms into account.

Grid-connected system: annual balance

The grid supplies the missing energy when the wind is weak and takes the surplus when it is strong. There is therefore no need to cover the least windy month; the aim is the balance over the year:

N = ⌈ 365·B′d / (Eyr·ηr) ⌉

Profitability (tab 6) then decides whether more or fewer machines than this number are worth installing.

Stand-alone system: four criteria

Without a grid, energy must be available when it is consumed. But the wind can drop for several days: the battery must bridge these calm periods. The number of machines and the battery size are linked: a large battery allows fewer machines, and vice versa. NRJXpert offers four criteria (tab 5, “Sizing criterion”):

B′bat equals B′d, or the priority share of B′d if the battery only backs up the priority loads. The cheapest battery of the database (or the imposed one) is kept; its recommended depth of discharge replaces the project value. At most DoD of the rated energy is drawn, and the efficiency ηbat covers charge and discharge losses.

Hour-by-hour simulation

The simulation follows the measurement series in chronological order (speeds extrapolated to the hub). At each step Δt, with Emin = Cnom·(1 − DoD), Emax = Cnom, and the battery full at the start:

if N·Pprod,1(t) ≥ Pload(t)/ηr:   charge E ← min(Emax, E + √ηbat·surplus·Δt); the rest is surplus (lost)
otherwise:   discharge min(shortfall, √ηbat·(E − Emin)); what cannot be supplied is a deficit
LPSP = Σ deficit / Σ demand     τ = 1 − LPSP

The battery efficiency is split between charge and discharge (√ηbat each). If the battery only backs up the priority loads, the others are shed as soon as the battery falls below half of its usable energy and the wind is not enough.

Cost / reliability optimisation (LPSP method)

This is the reference method for stand-alone systems (Borowy and Salameh, 1996; Kaldellis, 2002; Diaf et al., 2007; Yang et al., 2008). It looks for the cheapest system meeting a given reliability:

  1. For each autonomy of the list 6, 12, 18, 24, 36, 48, 72, 96, 120 h, 7, 10, 14 and 20 days: corresponding battery, then smallest number N of machines such that LPSP ≤ 1 − τtarget (hourly simulation).
  2. The pairs (autonomy, N) obtained form the sizing curve: N decreases as the battery grows.
  3. For each pair, discounted life-cycle cost LCC with the prices of tab 6 (machines, towers, electronics, batteries, installation, operation, replacement of batteries and electronics, residual value).
  4. The pair with the lowest LCC is kept; the “Autonomy” field is greyed out and shows the optimal autonomy.
  5. Sensitivity: the optimum is recomputed for τtarget = 90, 95, 98 and 99 %. The last hours of calm are very expensive: above 95 %, a back-up source (PV, generator) is often cheaper than extra machines and batteries.
Overproduction. A stand-alone wind-only system always produces much more than the demand: it is sized for the weak periods and produces too much in windy months. The surplus is unavoidable; it is used for deferrable uses (pumping, cooling, desalination) or reduced with a hybrid wind-PV system, the sun often making up for the low-wind months.

  4. the power that increases
Voc decreases by about 0.3 %/°C; Isc increases very slightly.

  1. the peak power
  2. the inverter losses
  3. the night energy only
  4. successive days without sun
Night energy is covered by the daily cycle; autonomy covers the overcast days.

  3. 4
  4. 8
